# Content Review: Rule the Empire

This document lists every decision in the game so you can spot-check it. It is generated from the content files by `npm run review-doc`, so it always matches what students see.

**How to read it:** each empire has 12–15 decisions, but a student sees only about 8 in one play-through. Options are labeled A/B/C here; in the game they appear in a random order. "Next" shows where each choice leads. "Stability −1" means the choice weakens the empire; if stability runs out, the empire collapses. "Flags" are earlier choices the game remembers to change later situations.

**Scoring rules:** Easy: 0 to +5 points per decision, starting stability 4; Medium: -1 to +6 points per decision, starting stability 3; Hard: -3 to +8 points per decision, starting stability 2. If your empire collapses, you lose half of your points (at least 10).

## Flagged for teacher review (uncertain or debated facts)

The game's content was checked line by line by an independent review pass, and clear errors were corrected.
The items below are **not known to be wrong**, but historians debate them, sources disagree, or the game
simplifies them. Spot-check these first.

### Ottoman Empire
- **"Millet system."** Many historians (e.g., Benjamin Braude) argue there was no formal, empire-wide millet system under Mehmed II; the term mostly dates from the 1800s. The game says "later called the millet system."
- **"Gunpowder empires."** The Hodgson/McNeill idea is standard in AP World but debated; guns were one factor among many.
- **Orban "from Hungary."** His origin is uncertain (Brassó in Transylvania, then part of Hungary; possibly German or Wallachian background).
- **Mamluks "look down on guns."** A classic thesis (Ayalon); by 1516 the Mamluks did have some guns.
- **Franco-Ottoman alliance "1536."** Contacts began in 1525; the date of the formal agreement is debated (1535/1536).
- **Osman II "planned to replace" the janissaries.** Based on chroniclers and rumor; his exact plans are debated.
- **Inflation from American silver.** Coin debasement in the 1580s is better documented; the game names both.
- **"Decline" framing (1600s–1700s).** Many historians now reject a simple Ottoman decline story; janissary power can be seen as a check on the sultan, and the military gap was more about organization and money than weapons.

### Safavid Empire
- **Peace of Amasya / capital move to Qazvin.** Sources date the move anywhere from about 1548 to 1558; the game says "the 1550s."
- **Shah Abbas's army.** Ghulam soldiers existed before Abbas; his musketeers were largely Iranian, and artillery was a separate corps. The game describes "ghulams, musketeers, and artillery."
- **Safavid "decline."** Abbas II (1642–1666) was capable; historians such as Rudi Matthee question a steady-decline story.
- **"End" of the Safavids in 1722.** Safavid shahs reigned in name until 1736 under Nader; the game says Safavid rule "effectively ended."

### Mughal Empire
- **Causes of revolts under Aurangzeb.** How much they came from religious policy (jizya 1679, temple destruction) versus money, land shortages, and succession disputes is debated. Note: Hindu nobles did not lose posts under Aurangzeb; their share actually rose (mostly Marathas joining).
- **Aurangzeb "never changed course."** Broadly true, though he suspended the jizya in the Deccan in 1704.
- **Din-i Ilahi.** Often described as a blend of religions; many historians see it as a small circle of disciples devoted to Akbar. The name appears only after his reign.
- **Deccan wars "emptied the treasury."** Standard view, debated alongside a crisis in land grants for nobles.
- **The "stay or go home" moment.** Babur's soldiers' homesickness crisis was in 1526; his famous morale speech was before Khanwa in 1527. The game combines them in one 1527 scene.
- **Taj Mahal dates.** "About 1632–1653" covers the whole complex; the tomb itself was done around 1643–1648.

### Ming & Qing China
- **The silver crisis.** The idea that falling silver imports in the 1630s–40s hurt the late Ming (Atwell) is debated (von Glahn and others); the game says "may have."
- **The 1721 missionary ban.** Kangxi's 1721 "ban" was a written note on papal documents; the formal ban came under his son Yongzheng in 1724. The game mentions both.
- **Walls vs. trade on the Mongol frontier.** Some historians (e.g., Arthur Waldron) credit the 1571 peace and border markets with Altan Khan more than the walls. The game gives walls 3 points and trade 2.
- **Population in 1644.** Estimates vary widely (about 100–160 million).
- **"Pirate King of Taiwan."** This reflects the Qing/European view; Koxinga was a Ming loyalist general and merchant-prince, which makes a good discussion point.
- **Jesuits "ever since Ricci."** Jesuit service at court was interrupted in 1664–1669 (the Calendar Case).

### Tokugawa Japan
- **"Sakoku" / "closed country."** The term was coined in 1801; historians (Toby, Arano) describe managed contact through four controlled gateways rather than isolation. The game notes Japan "was never completely closed."
- **Four-class order.** Samurai–farmer–artisan–merchant was a Confucian ideal, not a strict legal ranking; the game says "in Confucian theory."
- **Purpose of alternate attendance.** Whether draining daimyo wealth was the goal or a side effect is debated (Vaporis).
- **About 300,000 Christians.** Estimates range from about 300,000 to 370,000 or more.
- **Number of daimyo.** It changed over time (roughly 200–270+); the game says "about 250."
- **Rangaku and modernization.** That Dutch learning "helped Japan modernize quickly in the 1800s" is widely said but debated.

### Russian Empire
- **1480 as "the end of Mongol rule."** A standard convention; Ivan III hesitated, and the khan retreated partly because his Lithuanian ally never arrived.
- **When Moscow stopped paying tribute.** Probably in the 1470s; the exact date is debated.
- **The double-headed eagle.** The link to Byzantium through Sophia is the traditional story but isn't documented; some point to the Holy Roman eagle.
- **"Third Rome."** Historians disagree on how much this monk's idea shaped state policy.
- **"Ivan the Terrible."** *Grozny* is closer to "formidable" or "awe-inspiring."
- **"Soft gold."** A nickname used by historians; people at the time called furs "soft goods."
- **Start of Yermak's expedition.** Chronicles disagree (1579, 1581, or 1582).
- **How rich furs made the tsars.** Estimates of fur's share of state income vary (often about 10% in the 1600s).

### Spanish Empire
- **Moriscos expelled.** About 300,000 is a middle estimate (range about 270,000–320,000+); how much this hurt Spain beyond Valencia and Aragon is debated.
- **Jews expelled in 1492.** Estimates range from about 40,000 to over 100,000; the economic damage is debated.
- **"Most powerful empire in Europe."** Arguable, since the Crown went bankrupt repeatedly and relied heavily on Castilian taxes.
- **Silver and inflation.** The game says silver "helped cause" inflation; economists still debate how much.

### Songhai Empire
- **Askia Muhammad's caliph title.** Sources disagree on who granted it (the Abbasid caliph in Cairo or the Sharif of Mecca), and some scholars argue the chronicle passages were added later. The game says he was "named the caliph's deputy for West Africa."
- **Hajj dates.** Usually 1496–1497; some sources say 1497–1498.
- **"Standard weights and measures."** Often repeated in textbooks; the chronicles give little detail.
- **Sonni Ali as a "tyrant."** That picture comes from Timbuktu chroniclers who favored the Askias; Songhai oral tradition remembers him as a hero. (The game uses the spelling "Sonni" so students don't confuse his family name with Sunni Islam.)
- **The desert wells (1590).** Accounts differ on whether Askia Ishaq II ignored advice to block them or acted too late.
- **The cattle stampede at Tondibi.** One chronicle says gunfire turned the herd back into Songhai's lines; another says they scattered.
- **"That gap proved fatal."** The lack of guns is the standard explanation, but historians also stress the 1588 civil war, famine, disease, and internal divisions.
- **Army sizes.** About 4,000 Moroccan fighters is standard; Songhai estimates run from about 28,000 to 40,000+.

### Counterfactual options (by design)
Some top-scoring options are "the road not taken" (for example, Spain not sending the Armada, the Safavids training their princes, Songhai avoiding battle at Tondibi). These reward what would have been smartest for the empire; each explanation says clearly what actually happened.

## Mughal Empire

**Tier:** Easy (0 to +5 points per decision, starting stability 4). **Years:** 1526–1750.  
**Why easy:** Rich lands and brilliant early emperors. Rule wisely and tolerantly, and you'll thrive.  
**Intro shown to students:** You begin as Babur in 1526, a prince from Central Asia invading northern India with cannons and guns, and continue through his descendants Akbar, Shah Jahan, and Aurangzeb. Your strengths: some of the richest farmland and trade on Earth. Your challenge: a Muslim dynasty ruling over a mostly Hindu population.

**Best possible path (40 points):** The Battle of Panipat (A) → Stay or Go Home? (A) → Akbar and the Rajputs (A) → The House of Worship (A) → Running a Giant Empire (A) → A Monument for the Ages (A) → Aurangzeb's Choice (A) → War in the Deccan (A)

**If the empire survives:** Your Mughal Empire endures, rich and powerful, into the 1700s.  
**If it collapses (default text):** Rebellious nobles, angry subjects, and an empty treasury tore your empire apart. Rival kingdoms divided northern India.

### Real-history timeline (end screen)

- **1526:** Babur defeats the Sultan of Delhi at Panipat with cannons and matchlock guns, founding the Mughal Empire.
- **1540–1555:** Humayun is driven out of India by Sher Shah Suri, then returns with Persian help.
- **1556–1605:** Akbar allies with the Rajputs, abolishes the jizya (1564), builds the mansabdar system, and promotes "peace with all."
- **1632–1653:** Shah Jahan builds the Taj Mahal, a symbol of Mughal wealth and power.
- **1658–1707:** Aurangzeb expands the empire to its largest size, but brings back the jizya (1679) and fights endless wars in the Deccan.
- **1739:** Nader Shah of Persia sacks Delhi and carries off the Peacock Throne, speeding the empire's breakup. The British East India Company soon rises in its place.

### 1527 · Stay or Go Home?

<sub>id: `stay`</sub>

> You now hold Delhi, Agra, and the treasure of the Delhi sultans. But your soldiers hate India's heat and want to take their loot home to Kabul. Meanwhile, Rana Sanga, the Rajput king of Mewar, is gathering a huge alliance of Rajputs and Afghans to drive you out.

**A.** Give a rousing speech, promise rewards, and stay to build an empire in India. Then beat the Rajputs with the same guns-and-carts tactics.  
**+5 points** · next: `akbar`  
_What actually happened:_ That's what Babur did. He persuaded his men to stay and beat Rana Sanga's army at the Battle of Khanwa in 1527 using guns behind carts. Babur died in 1530; his son Humayun lost the empire in 1540 but regained it in 1555, and the dynasty lasted until 1857.

**B.** Take the treasure and go home to Kabul.  
**0 points** · stability -1 · next: `akbar` · sets flag: went_home  
_What actually happened:_ You'd win a fortune but lose an empire. Babur chose to stay, and India's wealth made the Mughals one of the richest dynasties in the world.

**C.** Stay, but try to buy off the Rajputs with gold instead of fighting.  
**+2 points** · next: `akbar`  
_What actually happened:_ Friendship with the Rajputs would matter a lot later, but in 1527 Rana Sanga wanted to drive Babur out entirely. Babur defeated him at the Battle of Khanwa that year.

### 1527 · The Rajput Challenge

<sub>id: `khanwa`</sub>

> Your hold on northern India is shaky. Rana Sanga, the Rajput king of Mewar, has gathered a huge alliance of Rajputs and Afghans to drive you out. Your soldiers are frightened; they have heard that Rajput warriors never retreat.

- _Extra line shown (only if: charged):_ Your reckless charge at Panipat won the day, but it cost you many of your best soldiers.
- _Extra line shown (only if: retreated):_ After retreating, you came back a year later and finally took Delhi, but you lost precious time.

**A.** Build a fortified line of carts, cannons, and matchlock gunners, and let the Rajputs charge into your guns.  
**+5 points** · next: `akbar`  
_What actually happened:_ Exactly Babur's tactic. At the Battle of Khanwa in 1527, his guns and flanking cavalry broke Rana Sanga's army. This victory made Babur the strongest ruler in northern India, though his son Humayun lost it in 1540 and only won it back in 1555.

**B.** Make a deal: give the Rajputs half your new lands if they leave you alone.  
**+1 points** · next: `humayun`  
_What actually happened:_ Giving away half your conquest so early would signal weakness and invite more attacks. Babur instead fought and won at Khanwa in 1527.

**C.** Pull back to Delhi and let the Rajputs wear themselves out.  
**0 points** · stability -1 · next: `humayun`  
_What actually happened:_ Waiting lets your enemies grow stronger. Babur faced the Rajputs head-on at Khanwa in 1527 and won with his guns.

### 1540 · Driven Out!

<sub>id: `humayun`</sub>

> Your weak start has caught up with your family. Babur's son Humayun is now emperor, and in 1540 an Afghan leader named Sher Shah Suri defeats him and seizes northern India. Humayun flees west with only a few followers. The Mughal Empire is close to disappearing.

**A.** Take refuge with the Shah of Persia, borrow Persian troops, and return to win back the empire.  
**+3 points** · next: `akbar`  
_What actually happened:_ This is what really happened (even though Babur had won his battles). Humayun lived in exile with the Safavid Shah Tahmasp, returned with Persian troops to take Kabul in 1545, and finally retook Delhi in 1555, shortly before he died. Persian artists and officials who joined him shaped Mughal culture for centuries.

**B.** Give up and retire to Kabul as a minor prince.  
**0 points** · stability -1 · next: `akbar`  
_What actually happened:_ That would end the Mughal story. Humayun refused to quit: he went into exile in Persia, came back with Persian troops, and recaptured Delhi in 1555.

**C.** Hide in the deserts of Sindh and raid Sher Shah's lands.  
**+1 points** · next: `akbar`  
_What actually happened:_ Raiding could keep your name alive, but not win back an empire. Humayun eventually got real help from Persia's Shah Tahmasp and retook Delhi in 1555.

### 1562 · Akbar and the Rajputs ★ turning point (every player sees this)

<sub>id: `akbar`</sub>

> You are Akbar, Babur's grandson, who became emperor at just 13 years old in 1556. You are a Muslim ruler, but most of your subjects are Hindus, and the proud Rajput kings control key forts and the roads to the sea. Your Muslim nobles expect you to keep collecting the jizya from Hindus. How will you win over, or win against, the people you rule?

- _Extra line shown (only if: went_home):_ Because Babur went home to Kabul, your family had to fight its way back into India, and many Indians still see you as foreign invaders.

**A.** Build alliances with Rajput kings: marry into their families, give them high ranks in your army, and abolish the jizya on Hindus.  
**+5 points** · next: `ibadat` · sets flag: tolerance  
_What actually happened:_ Brilliant, and Akbar's real policy. He married a Rajput princess in 1562, made Rajput kings top generals and governors, and abolished the jizya in 1564. Many Rajputs became the empire's most loyal defenders.

**B.** Conquer the Rajputs by force and keep collecting the jizya.  
**+2 points** · next: `chittor`  
_What actually happened:_ Force alone was costly. Akbar did besiege Rajput forts that refused his alliances, but his main strategy was partnership, including abolishing the jizya in 1564. That turned rivals into loyal commanders.

**C.** Require all of your subjects to convert to Islam.  
**0 points** · stability -1 · next: `chittor`  
_What actually happened:_ That would have turned millions of Hindus against you. Akbar did the opposite, abolishing the jizya in 1564 and welcoming Hindus into high government posts.

<sub>id: `chittor`</sub>

> Many Rajput kings refuse to bow to you. The great fortress of Chittor, home of the Rajput kingdom of Mewar, has closed its gates. Your army has cannons and skilled siege engineers, but every month of siege costs money, and the Rajputs are famous for fighting to the last.

**A.** Take the fort, then offer every other Rajput king a generous alliance: high rank, marriage ties, and an end to the jizya.  
**+5 points** · next: `mansabdar` · sets flag: tolerance

**B.** Take the fort, then destroy every Rajput kingdom one by one.  
**+1 points** · next: `mansabdar`  
_What actually happened:_ Endless war against proud warriors would drain your treasury. Even after Chittor fell in 1568, Mewar kept resisting for decades, so Akbar relied on alliances with other Rajput kings.

**C.** Give up the siege and avoid the Rajputs entirely.  
**0 points** · stability -1 · next: `mansabdar`  
_What actually happened:_ Leaving powerful enemies untouched near your capital is dangerous. Akbar took Chittor in 1568 and then won over most Rajput kings through alliances.

### 1575 · The House of Worship

<sub>id: `ibadat`</sub>

> Your new capital, Fatehpur Sikri, is a showcase of red sandstone palaces. You are curious about every religion in your empire, so you build a hall called the House of Worship. At first only Muslim scholars debate there, but soon you invite Hindus, Jains, Zoroastrians, and even Jesuit priests from Portuguese Goa.

**A.** Hold open debates among all faiths, and promote a policy of 'peace with all' (sulh-i kul) that treats every religion with respect.  
**+5 points** · next: `mansabdar` · sets flag: sulh  
_What actually happened:_ Exactly Akbar's approach. His debates and his policy of sulh-i kul helped hold a very diverse empire together. He also created a small spiritual circle called Din-i Ilahi for a few nobles, but he never forced it on anyone.

**B.** Create a brand-new religion that combines all faiths, and order everyone to join it.  
**+1 points** · next: `mansabdar`  
_What actually happened:_ Too far. Akbar's small spiritual circle, Din-i Ilahi, was only for a few loyal nobles and faded after he died. Forcing everyone to join would have angered Muslims and Hindus alike.

**C.** Close the hall. Only Sunni scholars should advise the emperor.  
**+2 points** · next: `mansabdar`  
_What actually happened:_ That would please some strict scholars but alienate the Hindu and Shia nobles your empire depended on. Akbar instead opened the debates to every faith and promoted "peace with all."

### 1580 · Running a Giant Empire ★ turning point (every player sees this)

<sub>id: `mansabdar`</sub>

> Your empire now stretches across northern India and is home to many millions of people. You need officers, soldiers, and a way to collect taxes from millions of farmers without letting nobles grow too powerful. Your finance minister, Raja Todar Mal, a Hindu, has an idea: measure the land and set taxes based on average harvests.

- _Extra line shown (only if: tolerance):_ Your Rajput allies now lead some of your best armies.

**A.** Give nobles, Muslim and Hindu alike, official ranks called mansabs. Pay them with land-tax rights that move every few years, and use Todar Mal's survey to set fair taxes.  
**+5 points** · next: `taj`  
_What actually happened:_ This is Akbar's real system. Mansabdars supplied soldiers and were paid from land taxes they could not keep forever, so no noble could build a private kingdom. Local landholders called zamindars collected the taxes, based on Todar Mal's careful surveys.

**B.** Give each noble a permanent family estate to rule however he likes.  
**+1 points** · next: `taj`  
_What actually happened:_ Permanent estates would let nobles build private power bases and someday challenge you. Akbar made sure mansabdars were moved around and could not pass their land rights to their sons.

**C.** Set one high tax on every farm, whether the harvest is good or bad.  
**0 points** · stability -1 · next: `taj`  
_What actually happened:_ Flat high taxes would ruin farmers in bad years and spark revolts. Todar Mal's system based taxes on surveys of land and average crop yields, which made collection fairer and more reliable.

### 1632 · A Monument for the Ages ★ turning point (every player sees this)

<sub>id: `taj`</sub>

> You are Shah Jahan, Akbar's grandson. Your empire is one of the richest on Earth, and its cotton cloth is sold from Southeast Asia to Europe. In 1631 your beloved wife, Mumtaz Mahal, died. You want to honor her and show the world your dynasty's glory. Your treasury is full, but some advisers want the money spent on conquering your ancestors' homeland in Central Asia.

**A.** Build a magnificent white marble tomb, the Taj Mahal, and a grand new capital at Delhi, while keeping the empire's finances in order.  
**+5 points** · next: `aurangzeb`  
_What actually happened:_ Monuments like these showed the power and legitimacy of Mughal rule. Shah Jahan built the Taj Mahal (about 1632–1653), which blends Persian, Islamic, and Indian styles, plus a new capital at Delhi with the Red Fort and the jeweled Peacock Throne.

**B.** Skip the buildings and spend the money conquering Central Asia.  
**+1 points** · next: `aurangzeb`  
_What actually happened:_ Shah Jahan did try this in 1646–1647: his armies took the city of Balkh in 1646 but had to withdraw in 1647 after huge expenses. The Taj Mahal, which he also built, proved a far better investment in Mughal prestige.

**C.** Save all the money. Big buildings are a waste.  
**+2 points** · next: `aurangzeb`  
_What actually happened:_ Saving is sensible, but monuments helped legitimize rule by showing power, faith, and wealth. Shah Jahan's Taj Mahal (about 1632–1653) still symbolizes the Mughal golden age.

### 1679 · Aurangzeb's Choice ★ turning point (every player sees this)

<sub>id: `aurangzeb`</sub>

> You are Aurangzeb, Shah Jahan's son. You seized the throne in 1658 after a bloody war against your brothers and imprisoned your father. You are a devout Sunni Muslim who wants your empire to follow strict Islamic law, and religious advisers urge you to bring back the jizya on Hindus. Meanwhile, the Hindu Marathas are rising in the south, and the Sikhs are growing stronger in the Punjab.

- _Extra line shown (only if: tolerance):_ For over a century, Akbar's alliances with the Rajputs have kept the peace and filled your armies with loyal Hindu commanders.
- _Extra line shown (only if NOT: tolerance):_ The Rajput kings have never fully trusted your dynasty.
- _Extra line shown (only if: sulh):_ Akbar's policy of 'peace with all' is still remembered fondly by Hindus, Sikhs, and Shia Muslims.

**A.** Keep Akbar's tolerant policies: no jizya, no temple destruction, and respect for all faiths.  
**+5 points** · next: `deccan`  
_What actually happened:_ This would have kept your Hindu allies loyal. Instead, Aurangzeb brought back the jizya in 1679 and destroyed some Hindu temples, which angered Rajputs, Marathas, and Sikhs and fueled revolts that weakened the empire.

**B.** Bring back the jizya and enforce strict Islamic law across the empire.  
**0 points** · stability -1 · next: `revolts`  
_What actually happened:_ This is what Aurangzeb really did in 1679. He also executed the Sikh leader Guru Tegh Bahadur (1675) and destroyed some Hindu temples. These policies turned many former allies into enemies.

**C.** Live by strict Islamic rules in your own court, but don't tax or punish non-Muslims for their faith.  
**+3 points** · next: `deccan`  
_What actually happened:_ A workable middle path: a ruler could be personally devout without alienating his subjects. Aurangzeb instead brought back the jizya in 1679, and resentment helped fuel the revolts that weakened Mughal rule.

### 1690 · War in the Deccan

<sub>id: `deccan`</sub>

> Your empire has reached its largest size ever, covering almost all of India. But in the Deccan, the Maratha kingdom founded by the warrior-king Shivaji keeps fighting. The Marathas avoid big battles; they raid supply lines and slip away into the hills. Your generals say victory needs just a few more years.

**A.** Make peace: let Maratha leaders rule their lands as your allies, paying tribute and sending you soldiers.  
**+5 points** · next: END (survived)  
_What actually happened:_ A realistic choice that would have saved men and money. Instead, Aurangzeb spent his last 26 years fighting in the Deccan; the endless war emptied the treasury, and after his death in 1707 the empire began to break apart.

**B.** Keep fighting until every Maratha fort is taken, however long it takes.  
**0 points** · stability -1 · next: END (survived)  
_What actually happened:_ This is what Aurangzeb did, staying in the Deccan from 1681 until his death in 1707. The war drained the treasury while the Marathas grew stronger, and after 1707 Mughal power declined quickly.

**C.** Pull your armies back and defend only the north.  
**+2 points** · next: END (survived)  
_What actually happened:_ Safer than endless war, but giving up the south without a deal could encourage other rebels. Aurangzeb chose to fight on until his death in 1707, a costly decision that weakened the empire.

### 1690 · Revolts Everywhere

<sub>id: `revolts`</sub>

> Your strict policies have stirred up resistance across the empire. The Marathas raid in the south, Rajput kings have rebelled, Sikhs are arming themselves in the Punjab, and Jat farmers have revolted near Agra. Your treasury shrinks as the wars spread.

**A.** Cancel the jizya, stop destroying temples, and negotiate with the rebel leaders.  
**+4 points** · next: END (survived)  
_What actually happened:_ Returning to Akbar's approach would have calmed many rebels. Aurangzeb never changed course, and after his death in 1707 the empire weakened quickly; in 1739 the Persian ruler Nader Shah sacked Delhi.

**B.** Crush every rebellion with force, no matter the cost.  
**0 points** · stability -1 · next: END (survived)  
_What actually happened:_ Close to what Aurangzeb tried. Fighting rebels on every side drained the treasury, and after his death in 1707 Mughal power broke up into rival kingdoms.

**C.** Hand large regions over to governors and let them deal with the rebels.  
**+2 points** · next: END (survived)  
_What actually happened:_ This might keep some peace, but it lets governors become nearly independent rulers. That is exactly what happened after 1707, when provinces like Bengal and Hyderabad broke away.

### Key terms defined in the Mughal Empire story

- **Mughal**: the dynasty founded by Babur; the name comes from the Persian word for Mongol
- **matchlock**: an early gun fired by touching a burning cord to the gunpowder
- **Rajputs**: Hindu warrior clans who ruled kingdoms in northwestern India
- **sulh-i kul**: Akbar's policy of 'peace with all' religions
- **Din-i Ilahi**: a small spiritual circle of nobles devoted to Akbar as their guide
- **mansabs**: official ranks that told each noble how many soldiers to supply and how he would be paid
- **zamindars**: local landholders who collected taxes from farmers
- **Sikhs**: followers of Sikhism, a faith founded by Guru Nanak around 1500 in the Punjab that teaches devotion to one God

### Key terms defined in the Ming & Qing China story

- **Ming**: the Chinese dynasty that ruled from 1368 to 1644
- **Oirat Mongols**: a powerful Mongol people of the northern steppe
- **steppe**: the huge grasslands of Central Asia, home to nomadic horse riders
- **Great Wall**: long defensive walls along China's northern border
- **tribute missions**: visits by foreign envoys who brought gifts to the emperor and received gifts and trading rights in return
- **wokou**: pirates and smugglers, both Japanese and Chinese, who raided China's coast
- **Single Whip Reform**: a reform that combined many taxes and labor duties into one payment in silver
- **eunuchs**: castrated men who served the emperor inside the palace
- **civil service exams**: tests on Confucian classics used to choose government officials based on merit
- **Manchus**: a people from Manchuria, northeast of China, who founded the Qing dynasty
- **Qing**: the Manchu-led dynasty that ruled China from 1644 to 1912
- **Mandate of Heaven**: the belief that Heaven gives a good ruler the right to rule and takes it away from a bad one
- **Little Ice Age**: a period of colder weather, worst in the 1600s, that caused crop failures around the world
- **queue**: a Manchu hairstyle: the front of the head shaved and the rest worn in a long braid
- **Rites Controversy**: a dispute over whether Chinese Christians could take part in Confucian ceremonies honoring ancestors
- **tribute**: gifts and formal respect that foreign rulers offered the Chinese emperor, who often gave back even richer gifts

### Key terms defined in the Tokugawa Japan story

- **daimyo**: powerful regional lords who ruled their own lands with their own samurai
- **samurai**: Japan's hereditary warrior class, who served daimyo and the shogun
- **shogun**: Japan's top military ruler, who governed in the emperor's name
- **shogunate**: the government run by the shogun
- **Edo**: the Tokugawa capital, today's Tokyo
- **rōnin**: samurai with no lord to serve
- **sankin-kōtai**: 'alternate attendance': daimyo had to live in Edo every other year
- **sakoku**: 'closed country': the policy of strictly limiting contact with foreigners
- **Dejima**: a tiny artificial island in Nagasaki harbor where Dutch traders had to live
- **kabuki**: a popular, colorful style of Japanese theater
- **rangaku**: 'Dutch learning': the study of European science through Dutch books

## Ottoman Empire

**Tier:** Medium (-1 to +6 points per decision, starting stability 3). **Years:** 1453–1750.  
**Why medium:** A mighty gunpowder army, but powerful rivals in Europe and Persia demand careful balance.  
**Intro shown to students:** You begin as Sultan Mehmed II in 1453 and guide the Ottoman Empire through the reigns of Selim I, Suleiman the Magnificent, and beyond. Your strengths: an elite army armed with gunpowder weapons, and lands that sit between Europe and Asia. Your challenges: rivals on every border and millions of subjects of many different faiths to hold together.

**Best possible path (48 points):** The Walls of Constantinople (A) → A City of Many Faiths (A) → Showdown at Chaldiran (A) → Egypt and the Holy Cities (A) → Suleiman at the Gates of Vienna (A) → Suleiman the Lawgiver (A) → The Janissaries Grow Too Powerful (A) → Vienna, Once More (A)

**If the empire survives:** Your empire endures to 1750 as one of the great powers of Europe, Asia, and Africa.  
**If it collapses (default text):** Too many costly mistakes left your treasury empty, your soldiers unpaid, and your provinces in revolt. Rival empires carved up your lands.

### Real-history timeline (end screen)

- **1453:** Mehmed II conquers Constantinople with giant cannons. The city becomes the Ottoman capital, Istanbul.
- **1514–1517:** Selim I defeats the Shia Safavids at Chaldiran, then conquers Mamluk Egypt and becomes protector of Mecca and Medina.
- **1520–1566:** Suleiman the Magnificent wins at Mohács, besieges Vienna (1529), and issues law codes. Sinan builds the Süleymaniye Mosque.
- **1656:** After decades of janissary revolts and money troubles, the Köprülü grand viziers restore order.
- **1683–1699:** A failed second siege of Vienna leads to the Treaty of Karlowitz, the first major permanent loss of Ottoman land to European powers.
- **1720s:** Leaders begin borrowing European ideas, including the first Ottoman Turkish printing press. The empire lasts until 1922.

### 1453 · The Walls of Constantinople ★ turning point (every player sees this)

<sub>id: `constantinople`</sub>

> You are Sultan Mehmed II, just 21 years old. Constantinople, the capital of what is left of the old Byzantine Empire, sits in the middle of your lands and controls trade between Europe and Asia. Its thousand-year-old walls have stopped attackers for centuries. An engineer from Hungary named Orban offers to build you some of the biggest cannons the world has ever seen.

**A.** Hire Orban, cast giant bronze cannons, and attack the city by land and sea at the same time.  
**+6 points** · next: `istanbul` · sets flag: cannons  
_What actually happened:_ Brilliant—this is what Mehmed did. Orban's cannons pounded the walls for weeks, Ottoman ships were even dragged overland into the city's harbor, and Constantinople fell on May 29, 1453. Big guns like these are one reason historians call the Ottomans a "gunpowder empire."

**B.** Surround the city and wait for it to starve. No need to risk an attack.  
**+2 points** · next: `walls`  
_What actually happened:_ Patient, but slow. A blockade alone could take years, because Venetian and Genoese ships could slip in supplies by sea. Mehmed won quickly in 1453 by combining a blockade with massive cannon fire and a final assault.

**C.** Leave the city alone and conquer easier lands in Anatolia instead.  
**-1 points** · stability -1 · next: `walls`  
_What actually happened:_ A missed opportunity. An enemy capital in the heart of your empire could become a base for European crusades. Mehmed made Constantinople his first target, and its capture in 1453 shocked Europe and made his reputation.

**B.** Order every Christian and Jew to convert to Islam or leave.  
**-1 points** · stability -1 · next: `chaldiran` · sets flag: harsh

**+1 points** · next: `chaldiran` · sets flag: harsh  
_What actually happened:_ Partly workable, but you would lose skilled Greek, Armenian, and Jewish merchants and artisans. Mehmed deliberately resettled people of many faiths in the city, and Istanbul grew into one of the largest and most diverse cities in the world.

### 1683 · Vienna, Once More

### Key terms defined in the Ottoman Empire story

- **sultan**: the ruler of the Ottoman Empire
- **Byzantine Empire**: the Eastern Roman Empire, a Christian empire centered on Constantinople
- **janissaries**: the sultan's elite infantry soldiers, armed with muskets
- **devshirme**: taking Christian boys from Balkan villages, converting them to Islam, and training them as soldiers or officials loyal only to the sultan
- **millet system**: letting each religious community run its own affairs under its own leaders
- **Qizilbash**: Turkmen tribal cavalry loyal to the Safavid shah, named for their red caps
- **Mamluk**: a sultanate ruled by former slave-soldiers that controlled Egypt and Syria
- **kanun**: law codes issued by the sultan to work alongside Islamic religious law
- **grand vizier**: the sultan's chief minister
- **Holy League**: an alliance of Christian powers, mostly Catholic, organized with the Pope's support
- **tax farming**: selling the right to collect taxes to private collectors, who kept anything extra they squeezed out

### Key terms defined in the Spanish Empire story

- **Reconquista**: the centuries-long Christian campaign to retake Spain from Muslim rulers
- **Inquisition**: a court run by churchmen under the Spanish Crown's control that tried and punished people accused of heresy (beliefs against Church teaching)
- **Taíno**: the main Indigenous people of the Caribbean islands Columbus first reached
- **Holy Roman Empire**: a large group of mostly German-speaking states in central Europe under an elected emperor
- **conquistador**: a Spanish soldier-explorer who conquered lands in the Americas
- **Indigenous**: native to a place; here, the original peoples of the Americas
- **Council of the Indies**: the royal council in Spain that governed the American colonies
- **viceroyalties**: huge colonial regions ruled by a viceroy, who governed in the king's place
- **encomienda**: a grant giving a Spanish settler the right to demand labor and tribute from Indigenous villages
- **mita**: a forced labor draft that made Indigenous villages send workers to the mines
- **Potosí**: a silver-mining mountain and city in modern Bolivia
- **Manila galleons**: Spanish ships that sailed between Mexico and Manila in the Philippines
- **Armada**: Spanish for a large war fleet
- **Moriscos**: descendants of Spanish Muslims who had been forced to convert to Christianity
- **Thirty Years' War**: a long, destructive religious and political war in Europe (1618–1648)

## Safavid Empire

**Tier:** Hard (-3 to +8 points per decision, starting stability 2). **Years:** 1501–1750.  
**Why hard:** A new Shia state squeezed between Sunni giants. One wrong war could finish you.  
**Intro shown to students:** You begin as Ismail in 1501, a teenage religious leader who has just captured Tabriz and crowned himself shah, and guide the Safavid dynasty through its golden age under Shah Abbas and beyond. Your strengths: devoted warriors, a powerful religious identity, and some of the world's finest silk and art. Your challenges: the mighty Sunni Ottomans to the west, Uzbek raiders to the east, and ambitious chiefs at home.

**Best possible path (64 points):** A New Shah in Tabriz (A) → Trouble in the East (A) → The Ottomans Are Coming (A) → Suleiman Invades (A) → Shah Abbas Takes Charge (A) → Half the World (A) → The Shah's Sons (A) → The Afghan Challenge (A)

**If the empire survives:** Your Safavid Empire survives its crises and remains a great power in 1750.  
**If it collapses (default text):** Your army crumbled, rebels and invaders closed in, and the Safavid dynasty fell.

### Real-history timeline (end screen)

- **1501:** Ismail I founds the Safavid Empire and makes Twelver Shia Islam the state religion.
- **1514:** Ottoman cannons and janissaries crush the Safavid cavalry at the Battle of Chaldiran.
- **1555:** The Peace of Amasya sets a border with the Ottomans. In the 1550s the capital moves from Tabriz to Qazvin, farther from the frontier.
- **1588–1629:** Shah Abbas builds a new army of ghulams, musketeers, and artillery, wins back lost lands, takes Hormuz from Portugal with English help (1622), and makes Isfahan a dazzling capital funded by the silk trade.
- **1722:** Afghan rebels capture Isfahan after a long siege, effectively ending Safavid power (Safavid shahs reigned in name only until 1736).

## Songhai Empire

**Tier:** Hard (-3 to +8 points per decision, starting stability 2). **Years:** 1464–1600.  
**Why hard:** Trans-Saharan gold makes you rich, but a northern neighbor with guns is coming.  
**Intro shown to students:** You begin as Sonni Ali in 1468, the warrior-king who turned Songhai into an empire along the Niger River, and guide it through the reign of Askia Muhammad and his heirs. Your challenges: keeping the support of Muslim scholars and merchants, avoiding fights over the throne, and a rival to the north with guns.

**Best possible path (64 points):** The Conquest of Timbuktu (A) → The Siege of Jenne (A) → Askia Muhammad Takes the Throne (A) → Organizing an Empire (A) → Askia Daoud's Golden Age (A) → Who Will Rule Next? (A) → An Army Crosses the Sahara (A) → The Battle of Tondibi (A)

### Real-history timeline (end screen)

- **1468:** Sonni Ali conquers Timbuktu, a great center of trade and Islamic learning. Jenne falls around 1473.
- **1493:** Askia Muhammad seizes power, makes Islam central to his rule, and makes the hajj to Mecca (1496–1497).
- **1500s:** Appointed governors, a professional army, and Timbuktu's scholars help make Songhai one of the largest empires in African history.
- **1588:** A civil war between rival princes weakens the Songhai army.
- **1591:** Morocco's gun-armed army defeats Songhai at the Battle of Tondibi. Gao and Timbuktu fall, and the empire breaks apart.
