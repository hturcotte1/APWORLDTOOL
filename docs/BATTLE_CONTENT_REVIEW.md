# Content Review: Class Battle

This document lists every decision in the live **Class Battle** mode so you can spot-check it. It is generated from the content files by `npm run review-doc:battle`, so it always matches what students see. (The solo game has its own review: `docs/CONTENT_REVIEW.md`.)

**How a battle works:** 4 rounds. In each round every empire's Ruler, General and Vizier each get one decision, all at the same time. Options are labeled A/B/C here; each student sees them in a random order. "Flags" are earlier choices the game remembers: they add extra lines, swap options, or prepare an army for a clash. Lines marked "only if" appear only when that earlier choice was made.

**Scoring:** Easy: 0 to +5 points per decision, starting stability 5; Medium: -1 to +6 points per decision, starting stability 4; Hard: -3 to +8 points per decision, starting stability 3. When stability reaches 0, the empire falls at the end of the round and loses half its points (at least 10); its students play on in exile for half points.

**Clashes:** the General's clash option has a battle strength (0–3). Each preparation flag the team holds adds 1. The stronger side wins the prize region; on a tie, whoever holds it keeps it. A side with no team in the class plays its historical option; a fallen empire fights at strength 0.

## Flagged for teacher review (uncertain or debated facts)

The Class Battle content was written for this mode, then checked line by line by an independent review pass, and clear errors were corrected.
The items below are **not known to be wrong**, but historians debate them, sources disagree, or the game
simplifies them. Spot-check these first. (Items that also appear in the solo game are listed in `docs/ACCURACY_FLAGS.md`.)

### Mughal Empire
- **"Stay or go home" (1527).** Babur's soldiers' homesickness crisis was in 1526 and his famous speech before Khanwa in 1527; the decision combines them.
- **Babur's cart-and-gun line "borrowed from the Ottomans."** Babur himself calls it the Ottoman (Rumi) method; some historians stress Safavid or Central Asian channels.
- **Todar Mal "began his career serving Sher Shah Suri."** Widely repeated, but it rests mostly on later accounts.
- **Thomas Roe (1615–1619).** The English already traded at Surat from 1613; Roe sought a formal treaty, which Jahangir never granted.
- **The Rahimi (1613).** The pilgrim ship the Portuguese seized belonged to Jahangir's mother, and Jahangir punished the Portuguese for it.
- **"European silver poured into India" (1615).** True across the 1600s, but the flow was still modest in 1615.
- **Aurangzeb and 1679.** The Rajput war had several causes (the succession in Marwar mattered as much as the jizya), and the best-known temple destructions came in 1669–70, before the jizya. How many temples were destroyed, and why, is debated (e.g., Richard Eaton).
- **The jagir crisis** behind the Mughal decline (Satish Chandra, Irfan Habib) is the standard account, but debated.

### Ming & Qing China
- **Wang Zhen and Tumu (1449).** Blaming the eunuch Wang Zhen is the traditional account; the emperor and court politics share the blame.
- **The Single Whip (1581).** The reform spread province by province from the 1530s; 1581 is the usual date for its empire-wide adoption.
- **Silver and the fall of the Ming.** Hedged with "may have." The idea that a silver shortage helped bring the Ming down (William Atwell) is disputed (Richard von Glahn and others).
- **China's population in the 1640s.** Estimates vary widely (roughly 100–150 million).
- **The Great Clearance and the Zheng.** That defectors and a navy, not the coastal evacuation, defeated the Zheng is an interpretation; the evacuation's death toll is unknown.
- **"Manchu armies never stayed long" inside the Wall.** They did hold four cities for some months in 1630.
- **Verbiest's cannons.** Counts vary: about 130 in the 1670s and 300 or more around 1680–1681.

### Tokugawa Japan
- **Christians in Japan by 1614.** "About 300,000" is the usual figure; estimates range from about 200,000 to 500,000. Martyr counts also vary.
- **Daimyo punished after Sekigahara.** "Dozens" lost lands (often given as about 88).
- **Shimabara (1637–1638).** The shogunate army is often given as about 125,000; 27,000–37,000 defenders were killed.
- **Shakushain's War (1669).** Ainu fighters killed "hundreds" of Japanese (estimates about 270–350).
- **The 47 rōnin.** "A law against private feuds" simplifies things: registered revenge killings were sometimes legal.
- **Yoshimune and Dutch learning.** He let in Chinese-language books on European science in 1720; his order for officials to study Dutch came around 1740.
- **Edo's population around 1700.** "May have a million people": estimates run from about 0.8 to 1 million.

### Ottoman Empire
- **Orban's guns "bigger than any the world has seen" (1453).** Fine as his boast, but other giant bombards already existed. His origin is uncertain, so the game doesn't give it.
- **The Franco-Ottoman alliance.** Contacts began in 1525; the date of the formal agreement is debated, so the game says "the 1530s."
- **The 1589 janissary riot** over debased coins, when the sultan handed over two officials to be executed, comes from chronicle accounts. How much American silver drove Ottoman inflation is debated.
- **Osman II (1622).** His plans and the "pilgrimage" pretext come from rumor; accounts differ on where he was seized. "The first Ottoman sultan killed by his own soldiers" is the standard view.
- **Baghdad.** Sources differ between 1623 and early 1624 for its fall, so the game says "about fifteen years ago" in 1638.
- **The Treaty of Zuhab border (1639)** is "close to" today's Iran–Iraq border: a simplification.
- **Bonneval** (reformed the bombardiers in the 1730s) was a former French officer who came to the Ottomans on his own, not sent by France.
- **Karlowitz (1699)** as the "first major permanent loss" of land to European powers is the standard framing but a generalization.
- **Lepanto: "several Ottoman commanders" advised staying in harbor.** Rests on chronicle accounts of Pertev Pasha and Uluç Ali.

### Russian Empire
- **1480 as the end of Mongol rule.** The "standoff on the Ugra" date is conventional; Ivan III had already stopped paying tribute.
- **Yermak.** His start date is uncertain; the capture of Sibir's capital is dated 1582. The game's "soft gold" nickname for furs is a common modern phrase.
- **The oprichnina's toll** ("thousands") and the forbidden years' dates are estimates.
- **Tsaritsa Anastasia.** Ivan suspected poison (the game frames it that way); tests of her remains found high mercury, but whether she was poisoned is debated.
- **The 1648 Moscow riot.** Mainly against corrupt officials; the hated salt tax had already been repealed in 1647.
- **Peter the Great.** "More than a thousand" streltsy executed in 1698, and St. Petersburg's forced laborers "many of whom died," are estimates.
- **"That's how Russia became the world's largest country."** A simplification: Siberia is the bulk of it, but later expansion also counted.

### Spanish Empire
- **Numbers:** "tens of thousands" of Jews exiled in 1492; about 300,000 Moriscos expelled (about a third of Valencia's people); "tens of thousands" resettled from Granada (about 80,000 is often cited); Alba's Council of Troubles executing "more than a thousand"; Cortés's force of "about 500 Spaniards." All are estimates.
- **Silver and inflation.** That American silver "helped drive up prices" in Europe is the standard view, but its weight is debated.
- **1566 in the Netherlands.** The big tax fight (Alba's "Tenth Penny") came after 1567, so the decision is about the heresy laws and the provinces' rights.
- **Treasure fleets** sailed in regular convoys only from the 1560s, and after 1588 they sailed safely for decades (the first full capture was in 1628).
- **"The sun never set" on Philip's lands** is a later saying.

### Safavid Empire
- **Ismail "only 14" in 1501.** He was 13 or 14.
- **Shia scholars from Lebanon and Iraq.** Most arrived under Tahmasp, not Ismail.
- **Qizilbash officers murdered one of Ismail's Persian chief ministers** (Mirza Shah Husayn, 1523). Worth a spot-check.
- **Guns at Jam (1528)** and the start of the ghulam system with Tahmasp's captives: commonly stated, but sources are thin. Abbas's musketeers were largely Iranian peasants, separate from the ghulams.
- **The capital's move to Qazvin** is dated anywhere from about 1548 to 1558; the game says "the 1550s." The royal silk monopoly is dated to 1619.
- **Safavid "decline" and the end in 1722.** Historians such as Rudi Matthee question a steady-decline story; Safavid shahs reigned in name until 1736.

### Songhai Empire
- **Sonni Ali.** The Timbuktu chronicles picture him as a tyrant with "magical powers"; oral tradition remembers him as a hero.
- **The Jenne siege** ("seven years, seven months, and seven days") is labeled as legend, dated "around 1473."
- **Askia Muhammad.** The title of the caliph's deputy and his hajj dates; the Mossi campaign (1497–1498) called a holy war; Agadez paying tribute (from Leo Africanus); standard weights and measures.
- **Askia Musa** took power in 1528 (some sources say 1529) and was killed in a revolt in 1531.
- **Taghaza.** Moroccan raids in the 1550s; the salt workers moved first to a nearby site and, after Morocco's occupation in the 1580s, probably to Taoudenni.
- **Bornu "did hire Turkish musketeers"** refers to Mai Idris Alooma.
- **The 1588 rebel** is named as Muhammad al-Sadiq, Askia Ishaq II's brother and a top army commander. Worth a quick check.
- **Tondibi (1591).** Most sources give 13 March 1591 (one chronicle gives 12 April). About 4,000 Moroccan fighters is standard; Songhai estimates run from about 28,000 to 40,000+. The lack of guns is the standard explanation for the defeat, but historians also stress the 1588 civil war, famine, and divisions. Morocco's demand for Taghaza taxes and the "block the wells" advice come from the chronicles.

### Clashes and map scenes
- **Kandahar (1622).** The Mughal relief army never came because Prince Khurram (later Shah Jahan) refused to lead it; his open revolt began in 1623, after the city fell. In the map scene, the "court quarrels" marker sits at Agra as a symbol; Jahangir was mostly at Lahore or in Kashmir that year.
- **The Siege of Osaka (1614–1615)** is played as one clash; the usual estimate is about 100,000 defenders, many of them masterless samurai.
- **The Amur (1685–1689).** Albazin was besieged in 1685–1686; the Treaty of Nerchinsk followed in 1689. Russia was at war with the Ottomans and Crimean Tatars at the same time.
- **Clash strengths are a game device.** Which side wins depends on the students' choices and preparations, not on a historical calculation; every clash's explanation says what really happened.
- **Map scenes** are drawn schematically: arrows show the general direction of a march, and ovals mark regions, not borders.

### Counterfactual options (by design)
As in the solo game, some top-scoring options are "the road not taken" (for example, Spain calling off the Armada or keeping the Moriscos, Songhai buying guns or blocking the wells, the Mughals making peace with the Marathas). These reward what would have been smartest for the empire; each explanation says clearly what actually happened. Extra lines that appear only after a team's earlier choice (for example, "The shah never built a corps of ghulams…") describe the team's own game, not real history.

### Showdown at Chaldiran 1514

**Round I.** Prize: **Eastern Anatolia**.

#### Ottoman Empire's General: Showdown at Chaldiran (1514)

> Shah Ismail of Safavid Persia has made Shia Islam his official religion, and his preachers are winning followers among the Turkmen tribes of eastern Anatolia. Sultan Selim orders you east to stop him. Ismail's Qizilbash cavalry are fearsome horsemen who believe their shah cannot lose. Your janissaries carry muskets and you have cannons, but the march across the mountains is long.

**What really happened:** option A (used when no team plays this empire).  
**Preparation:** none.  
**If they win:** Your guns win the day, and eastern Anatolia becomes Ottoman land.  
**If they lose:** The Safavids hold on to eastern Anatolia, and your army marches home with little to show.

**A.** Bring every cannon. Chain the guns behind a wall of wagons with your musketeers, and let the Qizilbash charge into them.  
**+6 points** · battle strength 3  
_What actually happened:_ That's what happened at Chaldiran in 1514. Ottoman cannons and muskets tore apart the Qizilbash cavalry, Selim briefly took Tabriz, and the Ottomans soon took over eastern Anatolia.

**B.** Leave the slow cannons and supply carts behind and race east with your fast cavalry alone, so you can catch Ismail's army before it slips away into the mountains and the summer ends.  
**+1 point** · battle strength 1  
_What actually happened:_ Speed without firepower throws away your biggest advantage. The Qizilbash were superb horsemen; the Ottomans won at Chaldiran because their guns could stop a cavalry charge.

**C.** Stay home and send the shah rich gifts instead.  
**-1 point** · battle strength 0 · stability -1  
_What actually happened:_ Leaving Ismail's preachers free to stir up revolt among the Turkmen of Anatolia would have weakened your hold at home. Selim saw the Safavids as a threat to his faith and his throne, and chose war.

#### Safavid Empire's General: The Ottomans Are Coming (1514)

> The Ottoman Sultan Selim I, a Sunni who calls your shah a heretic, is marching east with musket-armed janissaries and many cannons. Your Qizilbash are brave horsemen with swords and bows, and they believe Shah Ismail cannot lose. Eastern Anatolia, home to many Turkmen followers of the shah, lies right in the Ottomans' path. Fight, or fall back?

**What really happened:** option C (used when no team plays this empire).  
**Preparation:** none.  
**If they win:** Selim's army is worn down and turns back. Eastern Anatolia stays Safavid.  
**If they lose:** The Ottomans win at Chaldiran and take eastern Anatolia from you.

**A.** Avoid a pitched battle. Fall back, burn the crops in Selim's path, and let hunger wear his army down.  
**+8 points** · battle strength 3  
_What actually happened:_ The smartest move, and the one later shahs relied on against Ottoman invasions. Ismail did burn land at first, but then fought at Chaldiran in 1514 and was badly beaten.

**B.** Strike first at dawn, before the janissaries can set up their guns.  
**+2 points** · battle strength 2  
_What actually happened:_ Some of Ismail's commanders urged a quick attack, but the shah let the Ottomans set up their defenses. A surprise strike might have done better, but the Ottoman guns were still a huge advantage.

**C.** Meet the Ottomans head-on in open battle on the plain. Your Qizilbash have never lost, the shah's blessing protects them, and retreating now would shame you before every tribe that follows you.  
**-2 points** · battle strength 1 · stability -1  
_What actually happened:_ This is what happened at Chaldiran in 1514, and it was a disaster. Ottoman cannons and muskets shattered the Qizilbash charge, and the Ottomans went on to take eastern Anatolia.

🗺 **Map scene ("What really happened" on the projector): "Showdown at Chaldiran" (1514).** In 1514, Ottoman cannons and muskets crushed the Qizilbash cavalry at Chaldiran. Selim briefly took Tabriz, and the Ottomans soon took eastern Anatolia.

### The Siege of Osaka 1614

**Round I.** Prize: **Osaka and Kansai**. The computer plays the Toyotomi at strength 2.

#### Tokugawa Japan's General: Osaka Castle (1614)

> Toyotomi Hideyori, son of the great warlord Hideyoshi, still holds mighty Osaka Castle, and tens of thousands of masterless samurai (rōnin) have gathered there. As long as the Toyotomi survive, enemies of the Tokugawa have someone to rally around. Osaka's walls and moats are among the strongest in Japan.

**What really happened:** option A (used when no team plays this empire).  
**Preparation:** none.  
**If they win:** Osaka falls, and the Toyotomi threat is gone for good.  
**If they lose:** Osaka Castle holds, and the Toyotomi remain a rallying point for your enemies.

**A.** Besiege Osaka in winter, agree to a truce that lets you fill in the outer moats, then attack again in summer.  
**+5 points** · battle strength 3  
_What actually happened:_ That's what Ieyasu did. The winter siege of 1614 ended in a truce, the Tokugawa filled in the moats, and in the summer of 1615 they took the castle; Hideyori died and the Toyotomi line ended.

**B.** Storm the castle walls at once, before more rōnin arrive.  
**+1 point** · battle strength 2  
_What actually happened:_ Direct attacks on Osaka's huge walls cost the Tokugawa heavy losses in 1614. Ieyasu won by combining force with a clever truce that stripped the castle of its defenses.

**C.** Leave Hideyori alone in his castle. Attacking the son of the great Hideyoshi could turn him into a hero, and the rōnin will drift away if you simply wait.  
**0 points** · battle strength 0  
_What actually happened:_ Waiting would have let the Toyotomi stay a rallying point for every enemy of the Tokugawa. Ieyasu chose to destroy them in 1614–1615.

🗺 **Map scene ("What really happened" on the projector): "The Siege of Osaka" (1614–1615).** Ieyasu besieged Osaka in the winter of 1614, filled in its moats during a truce, and took the castle in 1615. The Toyotomi line ended.

### The Battle of Lepanto 1571

**Round II.** Prize: **Mediterranean sea lanes**.

#### Ottoman Empire's General: The Fleet at Lepanto (1571)

> Your fleet has just helped conquer Cyprus from Venice. Now a Holy League of Spain, Venice, and the Pope has gathered more than 200 galleys off the coast of Greece to strike back. Your galleys are sheltering in the fortified harbor of Lepanto. Your admirals argue about whether to sail out and fight or to stay safe under the fortress guns.

**What really happened:** option B (used when no team plays this empire).  
**Preparation:** `fleet` (the navy you built earlier).  
**If they win:** Your fleet keeps control of the Mediterranean sea lanes.  
**If they lose:** The Holy League wins the battle and takes control of the sea lanes.

**A.** Keep the fleet in the fortified harbor under its shore guns and let the League waste the sailing season.  
**+6 points** · battle strength 3  
_What actually happened:_ Several Ottoman commanders gave this advice. Instead the fleet sailed out and lost most of its galleys near Lepanto on October 7, 1571, though the Ottomans rebuilt it within a year and kept Cyprus.

**B.** Sail out and attack at once, while your crews are confident after taking Cyprus.  
**+1 point** · battle strength 1  
_What actually happened:_ This is what happened at Lepanto in 1571. The League's gun-heavy galleasses and heavier galleys smashed the Ottoman fleet, but the empire rebuilt its navy within a year.

**C.** Send the fleet home to Istanbul for the winter. Ships are expensive, the sailing season is almost over, and the League's quarreling allies will probably go home on their own before spring.  
**-1 point** · battle strength 0  
_What actually happened:_ Pulling back would hand the sea to your enemies without a fight. The Ottomans had just won Cyprus from Venice and needed their navy to hold it.

#### Spanish Empire's General: The Holy League (1571)

> The Ottomans have seized Cyprus from Venice, and their galleys raid Christian coasts. Pope Pius V has formed a Holy League of Spain, Venice, and other Catholic states, and King Philip's half-brother, Don John of Austria, commands its fleet of more than 200 galleys. The Ottoman fleet is gathered near Lepanto, on the coast of Greece.

**What really happened:** option A (used when no team plays this empire).  
**Preparation:** `fleet` (the ships and crews you prepared earlier).  
**If they win:** The Holy League's victory gives you control of the Mediterranean sea lanes.  
**If they lose:** The Ottoman fleet keeps control of the sea lanes.

**A.** Seek battle at once. Put Venice's gun-heavy galleasses out in front to break the Ottoman line before the galleys close in.  
**+8 points** · battle strength 3  
_What actually happened:_ That's what Don John of Austria did on October 7, 1571. The galleasses wrecked the Ottoman formation and the League destroyed most of its fleet, though the Ottomans rebuilt their navy within a year.

**B.** Wait out the season and blockade the coast instead, since the allies quarrel and one defeat could break the League.  
**+1 point** · battle strength 1  
_What actually happened:_ The League's members did quarrel, and it broke up in 1573. But waiting would have let the Ottomans tighten their grip on Cyprus and the eastern Mediterranean.

**C.** Pull Spain's galleys out to fight pirates off North Africa.  
**-3 points** · battle strength 0 · stability -1  
_What actually happened:_ Spain cared most about North Africa, but abandoning the League would have left Venice to face the Ottomans alone. Lepanto was a rare chance to strike the Ottoman fleet at full strength.

🗺 **Map scene ("What really happened" on the projector): "The Battle of Lepanto" (1571).** On October 7, 1571, the Holy League destroyed most of the Ottoman fleet near Lepanto. The Ottomans rebuilt their navy within a year and kept Cyprus.

### The Siege of Kandahar 1622

**Round III.** Prize: **Kandahar**.

#### Mughal Empire's General: Kandahar Under Siege (1622)

> Shah Abbas of Persia has besieged Kandahar, the fortress city that guards the road between India and Persia. Emperor Jahangir orders Prince Khurram to march west and save it, but the prince refuses to go, fearing that rivals at court will steal his place as heir. The garrison is small and cannot hold out for long.

**What really happened:** option B (used when no team plays this empire).  
**Preparation:** `artillery` (the cannons you invested in), `mansabdars` (your well-organized officer corps).  
**If they win:** Kandahar holds, and the road into India stays in Mughal hands.  
**If they lose:** Kandahar falls to the Safavids.

**A.** Send a relief army now under a loyal general, and settle the quarrel over the heir once Kandahar is safe.  
**+5 points** · battle strength 3  
_What actually happened:_ That would have been wise. Instead the relief army never came, because Prince Khurram refused to go and then rebelled against his father, and Kandahar fell to Shah Abbas in 1622. The Mughals won it back in 1638 but lost it for good in 1649.

**B.** Wait for the prince to agree to lead the army.  
**+2 points** · battle strength 1  
_What actually happened:_ This is close to what happened. The quarrel with Prince Khurram held up the army, and Kandahar fell to the Safavids in 1622 while the Mughal court argued.

**C.** Let Kandahar go. It is far from India, it costs a fortune to defend, and the Persians will only keep coming back for it.  
**0 points** · battle strength 0 · stability -1  
_What actually happened:_ Kandahar guarded the trade and invasion route into India, so losing it cost the Mughals both safety and prestige. They spent years and huge sums trying to win it back.

#### Safavid Empire's General: The Road to India (1622)

> Kandahar, the fortress city on the trade road between Persia and India, has been held by the Mughals since 1595. Your spies report that the Mughal court is divided: Prince Khurram is quarreling with his father, Emperor Jahangir, over who will be the next emperor. Shah Abbas's new army is ready to march.

**What really happened:** option A (used when no team plays this empire).  
**Preparation:** `ghulams` (your loyal ghulam soldiers), `musketeers` (the musketeers you trained).  
**If they win:** Kandahar is yours, and with it the trade road to India.  
**If they lose:** The Mughals hold Kandahar.

**A.** Strike now with siege guns while the Mughals quarrel, and take Kandahar before a relief army can arrive.  
**+8 points** · battle strength 3  
_What actually happened:_ That's what Shah Abbas did. He besieged Kandahar in 1622 and took it after a short siege, while the Mughal relief army never came, because Prince Khurram refused to lead it.

**B.** Send ambassadors to Emperor Jahangir to ask for Kandahar's return. Persia once helped his family win back their throne, a debt he may honor.  
**+1 point** · battle strength 1  
_What actually happened:_ Letters and embassies had not won the city back before, and the Mughals had no intention of giving it up. Persian help to Humayun in the 1540s did not change that.

**C.** Ignore Kandahar and attack the Ottomans in Iraq.  
**-3 points** · battle strength 0 · stability -1  
_What actually happened:_ Abbas did take Baghdad from the Ottomans soon after, in 1623–1624. But the Mughal quarrel in 1622 gave him a rare chance in the east that would not last.

🗺 **Map scene ("What really happened" on the projector): "The Siege of Kandahar" (1622).** In 1622, Shah Abbas took Kandahar while the Mughal court quarreled over the next emperor. The Mughals won it back in 1638 but lost it for good in 1649.

### The Amur Frontier 1685

**Round IV.** Prize: **Amur valley**.

#### Russian Empire's General: Albazin on the Amur (1685)

> Russian Cossacks and fur traders have pushed across Siberia to the Amur River and built a fort at Albazin. The Qing emperor Kangxi says the Amur valley belongs to his empire. In 1685 his army forced Albazin to surrender, but the Cossacks soon came back and rebuilt it. Now a bigger Qing army is on its way.

**What really happened:** option A (used when no team plays this empire).  
**Preparation:** `cossacks` (the Cossacks who opened Siberia for you), `forts` (the forts you built in the east).  
**If they win:** Russia keeps the Amur valley.  
**If they lose:** The Amur valley goes to the Qing.

**A.** Hold Albazin as long as you can, then send envoys to make a treaty: give up the Amur valley for a fixed border and the right to trade with China.  
**+6 points** · battle strength 1  
_What actually happened:_ That's what happened. The Cossacks held Albazin through a long siege in 1686, and in 1689 the Treaty of Nerchinsk gave the Amur valley to the Qing but let Russian caravans trade with China.

**B.** Send every soldier Siberia can spare to hold the Amur, whatever it costs.  
**+1 point** · battle strength 3  
_What actually happened:_ Russia's forces in the Far East were tiny and thousands of miles from Moscow, while the Qing could bring large armies and cannons. A long war there would have drained Russia while it was also at war with the Ottomans and Crimean Tatars.

**C.** Pull the Cossacks all the way back to the Urals.  
**-1 point** · battle strength 0 · stability -1  
_What actually happened:_ Giving up Siberia would have thrown away its rich fur trade, one of Russia's main sources of wealth. Russia kept Siberia and gave up only the Amur valley.

#### Ming & Qing China's General: Russians on the Amur (1685)

> Russian Cossacks and fur traders have built a fort at Albazin on the Amur River, in lands the Qing see as their northern frontier, close to the Manchus' own homeland. The Cossacks demand furs from local peoples who pay tribute to the Qing. Emperor Kangxi now turns his attention north.

**What really happened:** option A (used when no team plays this empire).  
**Preparation:** `banners` (your strong Eight Banners army), `cannons` (the cannons cast for your army).  
**If they win:** The Amur valley becomes Qing land.  
**If they lose:** The Russians keep their hold on the Amur valley.

**A.** Besiege Albazin with a strong army and cannons, then use the victory to set a firm border with the tsar's envoys.  
**+5 points** · battle strength 3  
_What actually happened:_ That's Kangxi's real strategy. Qing forces besieged Albazin in 1685 and 1686, and in 1689 the Treaty of Nerchinsk, worked out with Jesuit priests as interpreters, gave the Amur valley to the Qing and opened limited trade with Russia.

**B.** Build forts along the river and wait. The Russians are far from home, and hunger will drive them out.  
**+1 point** · battle strength 2

In early 1593 a Ming army using heavy cannons retook Pyongyang, Korean admiral Yi Sun-sin's warships attacked Japanese supply fleets, and after Hideyoshi died in 1598 the Japanese went home. The war was costly for the Ming treasury, but it kept a Japanese army off China's border.

**B.** Stay out. Korea's war is Korea's problem, and your soldiers are needed against the Mongols in the north.  
**+2 points**  
_What actually happened:_ If Korea fell, a Japanese army would stand on China's border, and Hideyoshi openly planned to march on Beijing. Defending a loyal tribute state also showed that the emperor kept his promises.

**C.** Build a great fleet and invade Japan itself. Strike Hideyoshi at home, and his armies in Korea will have to sail back to defend him.  
**0 points**  
_What actually happened:_ Invading Japan across the sea would have been a huge gamble; both of the Mongol ruler Kublai Khan's invasion fleets had failed, in 1274 and 1281. The Ming fought the Japanese in Korea instead, close to their own border.

#### Grand Secretary: Taxes in Silver (1581)

> You are Zhang Juzheng, senior Grand Secretary to the young Wanli Emperor. Since 1567, licensed merchants may sail overseas from a port in Fujian, and silver pours in from Japan and, after 1571, from the Americas through Spanish Manila. Yet farmers still pay taxes in grain and cloth and owe forced labor on roads and canals, while rich landowners hide their fields from tax collectors.

**A.** Survey the land, then combine the grain, cloth, and labor duties into one tax paid in silver. Keep the licensed trade that brings the silver in.  
**+5 points** · sets flag: silver_tax  
_What actually happened:_ This is the Single Whip Reform, which Zhang Juzheng spread across China in 1581 after a national land survey. Taxes became simpler and the treasury filled, but China now depended on a steady flow of silver from Japan (carried mostly by Portuguese and Chinese traders) and from Spanish America.

**B.** Keep collecting grain, cloth, and labor the old way. Grain feeds the armies directly, and a tax in silver would leave China at the mercy of foreign mines and foreign ships.  
**+2 points**  
_What actually happened:_ Your worry had something to it, since China's silver did come from foreign mines. But the old system was clumsy and easy to cheat, so Zhang Juzheng chose the Single Whip Reform, turning most taxes into one silver payment.

**C.** Close the Fujian port and ban overseas trade again.  
**0 points** · stability -1  
_What actually happened:_ When trade was banned before 1567, merchants turned to smuggling and pirate fleets raided the coast. Legal trade cut piracy and brought in the silver that China's busy markets needed.

### Round III: The Mandate Passes (1618–1683)

#### Emperor: The Mandate of Heaven Passes (1644)

> Famine, rebellion, and a weak government brought down the Ming. In 1644 the rebel Li Zicheng captured Beijing, and the last Ming emperor hanged himself. A Ming general then joined forces with the Manchus at the Great Wall, and together they drove Li out. Now your Manchu family, whose Qing dynasty was proclaimed in Manchuria in 1636, rules from Beijing: a small minority ruling more than a hundred million Han Chinese.

- _Extra line shown (only if: silver_tax):_ Because Ming taxes were paid in silver, a drop in silver imports in the 1630s and 1640s may have made hard times worse for taxpayers.
- _Extra line shown (only if: wall):_ Manchu armies raided through the rebuilt Great Wall several times but never stayed for long, until the Ming general guarding Shanhai Pass joined them in 1644.

**A.** Keep the Ming system: Confucian ministries, the exams, and Han officials beside Manchus. Claim the Mandate of Heaven, and keep the Eight Banners as your core army.  
**+5 points** · stability +1 · sets flag: banners · gains: Manchuria  
_What actually happened:_ Exactly the Qing strategy. They kept the exams and ministries, often paired a Manchu and a Han official in top posts, and kept the Eight Banners as the army's core. But they also forced Han men to shave the front of their heads and wear the Manchu queue as a sign of loyalty, and many who refused were killed.

**B.** Govern only through Manchu nobles, and abolish the exams. Officials trained to serve the Ming will never truly serve a Manchu dynasty.  
**0 points** · stability -1  
_What actually happened:_ A small Manchu minority could never govern so many people alone. The Qing restarted the exams by 1646 and kept Han officials, which over time won over most of China's educated class.

**C.** Reward the Ming generals who joined you with huge southern provinces to rule as their own kingdoms.  
**+2 points**  
_What actually happened:_ After conquering the south, the Qing really did give three Han generals huge territories to rule. It worked at first, but in 1673 these "Three Feudatories" rebelled against the emperor.

#### General: Koxinga's Island (1662)

> You command the Qing forces on the southern coast. In 1662 the Ming loyalist Zheng Chenggong, known in Europe as Koxinga, drove the Dutch out of Taiwan, then died a few months later. His son now holds the island as a base, and the Zheng war fleets raid your coast. Your soldiers are horsemen and foot soldiers, not sailors.

**A.** Build a navy first. Then win over Zheng commanders willing to switch sides, and let them lead the attack on Taiwan.  
**+5 points** · gains: Taiwan  
_What actually happened:_ That's what the Qing did. Admiral Shi Lang, a former Zheng commander who had switched sides, led a Qing fleet to victory at the Penghu Islands in 1683, and Taiwan surrendered and became part of the Qing Empire.

**B.** Send your army across the strait at once in every boat you can seize. Each month you wait, the Zheng fleet burns another coastal town.  
**0 points** · stability -1  
_What actually happened:_ Without warships, an army crossing the stormy Taiwan Strait would be sunk by the Zheng fleet or the weather, and early Qing attempts in the 1660s failed. The Qing waited until it had a real navy, then won in 1683.

**C.** Leave Taiwan alone for now. It's a small island across a stormy strait, and your soldiers are needed on the mainland, where rebels still hold out.  
**+2 points**  
_What actually happened:_ For years the Qing did put the mainland first. But a hostile fleet just off the coast kept raiding, and in 1674 the Zheng joined a great revolt in the south and landed troops in Fujian.

#### Grand Secretary: Empty the Coast? (1661)

> As Grand Secretary, you must help cut off the Zheng fleets. They depend on the people of the southern coast for food, timber, sailors, and smuggled trade. Some officials propose a drastic order: force everyone along the coast to move inland, destroy the empty villages, and put to death anyone who crosses back. Others say Zheng commanders and coastal merchants can be won over.

- _Extra line shown (only if: silver_tax):_ With taxes paid in silver, coastal trade has become one of the empire's lifelines, and an empty coast would choke off much of the silver flowing into China.

**A.** Offer pardons, titles, and rewards to Zheng commanders and coastal merchants who come over to you. Meanwhile, build a navy of your own.  
**+5 points**  
_What actually happened:_ The Qing did win over many Zheng officers with titles and rewards, and Shi Lang, the admiral who finally took Taiwan in 1683, was one of them. In the end, it took defectors and a real navy to defeat the Zheng regime.

**B.** Clear the coast: move everyone inland, destroy the villages, and let no one return. Without food, sailors, or trade, the Zheng fleets will wither.  
**+2 points**  
_What actually happened:_ The Qing really did this, starting in 1661, in what is called the Great Clearance. Whole communities lost their homes, fields, and fishing boats, and many people starved; it hurt the Zheng, but the order was fully lifted only in 1684, after Taiwan fell.

**C.** Raise taxes on the coastal provinces to pay for the war, since the coast is where the trouble is.  
**+1 point**  
_What actually happened:_ The coastal provinces were already suffering from war and raids, and higher taxes would push more coastal people to join or trade with the Zheng. The Qing instead tried to cut the Zheng off from the coast and win over their officers.

### Round IV: Kangxi's Peace (1673–1722)

#### Emperor: The Rites Controversy (1720)

> You are the Kangxi Emperor, on the throne since 1661. You have studied geometry with Jesuit priests, and Jesuits run your astronomy office. But in 1715 the Pope ruled that Chinese Christians may not take part in ceremonies honoring their ancestors and Confucius. To you, those rites are the heart of Chinese culture and family loyalty. Now a papal envoy has come to Beijing to insist on the ruling.

- _Extra line shown (only if: cannons):_ Because your armies rely on cannons, the Jesuits have been extra useful: the priest Ferdinand Verbiest cast hundreds of guns for you.

**A.** Keep the Jesuit scientists at court. But ban missionaries who obey the Pope's ruling from preaching in China.  
**+5 points**  
_What actually happened:_ Close to Kangxi's response. In 1721, after reading the Pope's ruling, he wrote that Europeans should no longer preach in China, while Jesuit astronomers and artists kept serving at court. His son Yongzheng formally banned Christianity in 1724.

**B.** Accept the Pope's ruling and let missionaries preach freely. Their religion is their own business, and their science has served you well.  
**+1 point**  
_What actually happened:_ That would let a foreign religious leader decide what your subjects could do, a challenge to the emperor's authority. Kangxi refused and stopped missionary preaching in 1721.

**C.** Expel every European, the court scientists included. Foreigners who obey the Pope over the emperor cannot be trusted to run your calendar.  
**+2 points**  
_What actually happened:_ Too extreme for Kangxi, who valued Jesuit mathematics and astronomy. He stopped missionary preaching but kept useful Jesuit experts at court, and Jesuits served there for decades more.

#### Grand Secretary: The Three Feudatories Revolt (1673)

> As Grand Secretary to the young Kangxi Emperor, you face a crisis. Three Han Chinese generals rule huge southern provinces almost like kings, with their own armies and taxes. When Kangxi moves to abolish their domains, they rebel, and much of southern China joins them.

- _Extra line shown (only if: banners):_ Your emperor made the Eight Banners the core of the army, and they are ready to march south.

**+4 points**

**B.** Let the generals keep their lands forever. They helped your family conquer China, and a long civil war could cost the young dynasty everything.  
**0 points** · stability -1  
_What actually happened:_ That would leave China split into rival kingdoms. Kangxi refused, and his victory in 1681 made the Qing the unquestioned rulers of China.

**C.** Fight, and promise death to every rebel, even those who surrender. Mercy would only teach other generals that rebellion is cheap.  
**+2 points**  
_What actually happened:_ Kangxi accepted surrenders instead, and two of the three rebel leaders gave up in 1676–1677, which split the revolt (both were later put to death). Refusing all mercy would have forced every rebel to fight to the end.

## Tokugawa Japan

**Tier:** Easy (0 to +5 points per decision, starting stability 5). **Best possible team score:** 60.  
**Offices:** Ruler: Shogun · General: General · Vizier: Senior Councilor.  
**Regions on the map:** Kanto (around Edo) (held at the start), Tohoku (the north), Kyushu, Southern Ezo.

### Round I: Unification (1600–1615)

#### Shogun: A Title Fit for a Ruler (1603)

> You are Tokugawa Ieyasu. Japan suffered more than a century of civil war among its daimyo before the warlord Toyotomi Hideyoshi reunited it. After he died in 1598, the lords split into rival camps, and in 1600 you won the great Battle of Sekigahara. Now you hold the real power, but Japan also has an emperor in Kyoto, from a family said to descend from the sun goddess. He has little power but enormous prestige. Military rulers called shoguns have often governed in the emperor's name. How will you make your rule legitimate?

**A.** Have the emperor name you shogun. Keep him in Kyoto as an honored symbol, and govern from your castle town of Edo.  
**+5 points** · stability +1 · sets flag: shogun  
_What actually happened:_ Exactly right. In 1603 the emperor appointed Ieyasu shogun, giving his rule legitimacy while real power stayed in Edo. The Tokugawa shogunate ruled Japan until 1868.

**B.** Remove the emperor and take the throne yourself. The man who won the war and holds the real power deserves the highest title.  
**0 points** · stability -1  
_What actually happened:_ The emperor was believed to descend from the sun goddess, and replacing him would have turned many daimyo against you. Ieyasu was smarter: he ruled as shogun in the emperor's name.

**C.** Rule as guardian for Toyotomi Hideyori, the young son of the last great warlord. His father's old supporters would accept you, and you would still hold the real power.  
**+2 points**  
_What actually happened:_ Ieyasu had been one of five regents for Hideyori after Hideyoshi died in 1598, but staying a guardian would keep a rival family at the center of power. He took the title of shogun in 1603 and destroyed the Toyotomi at Osaka in 1615.

#### General: clash, The Siege of Osaka (1614)

See [The Siege of Osaka 1614](#the-siege-of-osaka-1614) above.

#### Senior Councilor: Redrawing the Map (1600)

> As Ieyasu's Senior Councilor, you must redraw Japan's map after the victory at Sekigahara. Each daimyo rules his own domain with his own samurai. Some fought against you, some switched sides at the last minute, and some are fudai, hereditary vassals whose families have served the Tokugawa for generations. The huge lands taken from the losers are yours to hand out. How will you arrange the lords?

**A.** Give fudai lands near Edo and along the main roads. Keep "outside" lords (tozama) far away, and strip or shrink your enemies' lands.  
**+5 points** · sets flag: loyal_map · gains: Tohoku (the north)  
_What actually happened:_ That's what Ieyasu did. He took away or shrank the lands of dozens of enemy daimyo, placed fudai near Edo, and kept the tozama, former enemies and newer allies alike, at a distance; in the north, the Uesugi clan that had opposed him kept only a quarter of its lands. This map of power lasted for more than 250 years.

**B.** Execute every lord who fought against you and seize all of his lands. A lord who is dead can never raise another army.  
**+1 point** · stability -1  
_What actually happened:_ Mass executions would terrify the surviving lords into new revolts. Ieyasu executed a few leaders, such as Ishida Mitsunari, but mostly took away or cut lands and moved rivals far from Edo.

**C.** Let every lord keep his lands as before. Generosity, not fear, will win their loyalty, and it will spare Japan another war.  
**+2 points**  
_What actually happened:_ Too soft. Leaving powerful enemies with their full lands and armies invites another civil war, so Ieyasu rearranged the whole map of Japan's domains to protect his power.

### Round II: Order (1614–1639)

#### Shogun: The Christian Question (1614)

> As shogun, you must decide the fate of Japan's Christians. Since Jesuit missionaries arrived in 1549, about 300,000 Japanese have become Christians, including some daimyo. Spanish friars have come too, from the Philippines, a land Spain conquered. Some advisers warn that missionaries are the first step toward European conquest and that converts will obey the Pope before the shogun. But the Portuguese ships that bring priests also bring Chinese silk that Japan's markets want.

**A.** Ban Christianity and expel the missionaries. Later, make every family register at a Buddhist temple to prove it is not Christian.  
**+5 points** · sets flag: ban  
_What actually happened:_ This is what the shogunate did: a nationwide ban in 1614, then rules forcing every family to register at a Buddhist temple. Thousands of Japanese Christians were tortured or killed for refusing to give up their faith, and others worshiped in secret for more than two centuries. Harsh as it was, the ban removed what the shogunate saw as a foreign threat to its control.

**B.** Welcome the missionaries. Christianity could bring European allies and guns, and Christian daimyo would owe you loyalty if a rival ever rose against you.  
**+1 point**  
_What actually happened:_ Risky for a new government: the shogunate feared that missionaries served Spain and Portugal and that converts would put the Pope above the shogun. It banned Christianity in 1614, and thousands of Japanese Christians were later persecuted or killed.

**C.** Leave things as they are. The Christians have caused no trouble, and the silk trade depends on good relations with the Portuguese.  
**+2 points**  
_What actually happened:_ The silk trade really did make officials hesitate, but the shogunate decided Christianity was too dangerous to tolerate. It banned the religion in 1614, and over the next decades thousands of Christians who would not give up their faith were persecuted or killed.

#### General: The Shimabara Rebellion (1638)

> You command the shogun's armies. Late in 1637, in the Shimabara region of Kyushu, peasants crushed by heavy taxes, famine, and their lord's cruelty rose up. Many are Christians, persecuted by that same lord. Led by a teenager called Amakusa Shirō, tens of thousands of rebels now hold an old castle by the sea, and the shogunate's first attacks on its walls have failed.

- _Extra line shown (only if: shogun):_ Because Ieyasu took the title of shogun, the Kyushu lords must send troops when you call, and they do.
- _Extra line shown (only if NOT: shogun):_ Because your ruler never took the title of shogun, some Kyushu lords are slow to answer your call for troops.

**A.** Surround the castle with a huge army from the Kyushu domains and starve the rebels out. Then punish the lord whose cruelty started it all.  
**+5 points** · gains: Kyushu  
_What actually happened:_ That's what happened. A shogunate army of more than 100,000 starved out the castle and took it in 1638, killing nearly all of the tens of thousands inside, including women and children. Afterward the lord, Matsukura Katsuie, was executed for misrule, a rare punishment for a daimyo.

**B.** Negotiate: let the rebels keep their faith if they go home. A long siege could cost thousands of soldiers, and peasants sent home can still farm and pay taxes.  
**+1 point** · stability -1 · sets flag: tolerated  
_What actually happened:_ Giving in to an armed revolt would encourage more of them. The shogunate crushed the rebellion in 1638, blamed Christianity for it, and enforced its ban on the religion even more harshly.

**C.** Crush the rebellion, but leave the local lord and his taxes alone.  
**+2 points**  
_What actually happened:_ Victory without fixing the cause invites new revolts. The shogunate did crush the rebels in 1638, but it also executed the local lord whose cruelty had caused the uprising.

#### Senior Councilor: Keeping the Daimyo in Line (1635)

> As Senior Councilor to Iemitsu, the third Tokugawa shogun, you face a quiet danger. Japan is at peace, but about 250 daimyo still rule their own domains, with their own castles and samurai. Many lords already visit Edo and leave relatives there as a sign of loyalty, but no law yet requires it. You need a system that keeps the lords loyal and unable to rebel, without fighting another war.

- _Extra line shown (only if: loyal_map):_ Because fudai lords hold the lands around Edo, every daimyo traveling to the capital must pass loyal castles and checkpoints.
- _Extra line shown (only if: shogun):_ Because Ieyasu became shogun, the daimyo have sworn oaths to obey the laws the shogun issues for the warrior houses.

**A.** Make every daimyo live in Edo every other year, and keep his wife and heir there for good.  
**+5 points** · stability +1 · sets flag: alternate  
_What actually happened:_ This is the real policy, called sankin-kōtai, made law in 1635 and extended to all daimyo by 1642. The trips to Edo with huge processions were very expensive, and the wives and heirs left in Edo were basically hostages, so rebellion became nearly impossible.

**B.** Station shogunate soldiers in every domain to inspect each lord's castle. Have them report at once on any daimyo who gathers troops or meets in secret with his neighbors.  
**+1 point**  
_What actually happened:_ The shogunate did send inspectors to the domains from time to time, but keeping soldiers in some 250 domains would have been hugely expensive. Sankin-kōtai worked far more cheaply, since the daimyo paid for their own costly trips to Edo.

**C.** Trust the daimyo. The wars are over, and treating loyal lords like suspects will only give them a reason to turn against you.  
**0 points** · stability -1  
_What actually happened:_ Too trusting. Lords with castles and armies could restart civil war, so in 1635 the shogunate made alternate attendance (sankin-kōtai) the law.

### Round III: The Closed Country (1639–1700)

#### Shogun: Closing the Country (1639)

> As shogun, you must decide what to do about the foreigners. The Shimabara Rebellion has convinced your government that Christianity, and the Europeans who bring it, are dangerous. The Dutch, Protestants who care about profit rather than converts, even lent a ship to fire on the rebels' castle. But the Catholic Portuguese bring much of the Chinese silk Japan buys, and they smuggle in priests too. Since 1635, Japanese have been forbidden to travel abroad.

- _Extra line shown (only if: ban):_ Christianity has been banned since 1614, and officials still hunt for Christians hiding their faith.
- _Extra line shown (only if: tolerated):_ Your general let the Shimabara rebels keep their faith, so Christians in Kyushu now worship openly, and Portuguese ships keep bringing priests.

**A.** Expel the Portuguese. Let only the Dutch and Chinese trade at Nagasaki, under close watch, and keep the trade with Korea.  
**+5 points** · sets flag: dejima  
_What actually happened:_ This was the policy later called sakoku: the Portuguese were expelled in 1639, and in 1641 the Dutch were moved to the tiny island of Dejima in Nagasaki harbor. Trade with China, Korea, the Ryukyu Islands, and the Ainu continued through approved channels, keeping foreign influence out while bringing in goods and news.

**B.** Any ship might carry a missionary, so cut off all foreign contact completely: no ships, no traders, no exceptions.  
**+2 points** · sets flag: closed  
_What actually happened:_ Japan was never completely closed. The shogunate kept carefully controlled trade with the Dutch and Chinese at Nagasaki and with Korea, which brought in silk, medicines, books, and news of the world.

**C.** Keep trading with the Portuguese, but search their ships for priests. Their silk is too valuable to lose, and the Dutch may not be able to replace it.  
**+1 point**  
_What actually happened:_ Portuguese silk really did make officials hesitate, but searches had never stopped priests from slipping in. The shogunate decided the Dutch and Chinese could supply such goods instead, and it expelled the Portuguese in 1639.

#### General: Shakushain's War (1669)

> You command the shogun's forces in the north. On the island of Ezo (today's Hokkaido), the Matsumae clan holds the shogun's permission to control all trade with the Ainu, the Indigenous people of Ezo. Matsumae traders keep giving less rice for the Ainu's salmon, furs, and other goods. Now the Ainu leader Shakushain has united many communities and attacked Japanese traders and sailors, killing hundreds. Matsumae has asked the shogun for help.

- _Extra line shown (only if: loyal_map):_ Because your Senior Councilor redrew the domains, the northern lords hold their lands at the shogun's pleasure, and they stand ready to send troops across the strait.

**A.** Order the northern domains to send troops and help Matsumae end the revolt. Then make Matsumae trade fairly, so the Ainu have no reason to rise again. _(only if: loyal_map)_  
**+5 points** · gains: Southern Ezo  
_What actually happened:_ The shogunate did order northern domains, especially Tsugaru, to help, and the revolt collapsed after Matsumae men killed Shakushain at a peace meeting in 1669. But fair trade is the road not taken: afterward Matsumae forced Ainu leaders to swear loyalty and trade on even worse terms, and Ainu communities grew poorer and more dependent.

**A2.** Ship your own soldiers north to help Matsumae end the revolt. Then make Matsumae trade fairly, so the Ainu have no reason to rise again. _(only if NOT: loyal_map)_  
**+4 points** · gains: Southern Ezo  
_What actually happened:_ Helping Matsumae was the right goal, but shipping troops so far was slow and costly; historically the shogun ordered the northern domains, especially Tsugaru, to send them. The revolt collapsed after Shakushain was killed at a peace meeting in 1669, and afterward the Ainu were forced to trade on even worse terms, not fairer ones.

**B.** Pull out of Ezo. The island is cold and far away, and it isn't worth a war.  
**+1 point**  
_What actually happened:_ Ezo's trade goods, such as dried salmon and furs, made Matsumae rich, and the shogunate would not give up its foothold in the north. It kept control, and after Shakushain was killed in 1669 the Ainu's trade terms got even worse.

**C.** Wage a long war to burn every Ainu village that joined Shakushain. Only a terrible lesson will stop the Ainu from ever rising again.  
**+2 points** · stability -1  
_What actually happened:_ A long war of revenge would cost lives and money on both sides and could unite even more Ainu against you. The real revolt collapsed in 1669, after Shakushain was killed at a peace meeting, but afterward the Ainu were forced to accept even worse trade terms.

#### Senior Councilor: Merchants Get Rich (1700)

> As Senior Councilor, you see how a century of peace has transformed Japan. Edo may have a million people, and Osaka merchants grow rich trading rice and lending money. City people crowd kabuki theaters and buy woodblock prints. But in Confucian theory, merchants rank lowest, below samurai, farmers, and artisans. And many samurai, paid fixed stipends in rice, are now deep in debt to merchants.

- _Extra line shown (only if: alternate):_ The costly trips to Edo required by sankin-kōtai have pushed many lords and their samurai to borrow heavily from merchants.

**A.** Keep the official class order on paper, but let the merchants prosper. Their trade feeds your growing cities.  
**+5 points**  
_What actually happened:_ This is roughly what happened, though the shogunate still lived mostly on rice taxes from farmers and taxed merchants only lightly. Merchants grew rich and city culture flourished, but the government was often short of money.

**B.** Cancel all samurai debts and seize the property of the richest merchants. Samurai are the backbone of your government, and merchants who grow fat on their debts have forgotten their place.  
**0 points** · stability -1  
_What actually happened:_ That would wreck trust and the economy that fed your cities. The shogunate sometimes helped indebted samurai and even seized one rich Osaka merchant's fortune in 1705, but it relied on merchants' wealth and kept them in business.

**C.** Let samurai give up their rank and become merchants.  
**+2 points**  
_What actually happened:_ That would break the class system that kept order. The Tokugawa kept samurai as a separate class of officials and warriors paid in rice, even though many grew poor.

### Round IV: Reform (1700–1750)

#### Shogun: Dutch Learning (1720)

> You are Tokugawa Yoshimune, the eighth shogun. You want practical knowledge to improve farming, medicine, and the calendar. But since 1630, foreign books that mention Christianity have been banned, and the ban has kept out even Chinese translations of European books on astronomy and mathematics. The Dutch and Chinese traders at Nagasaki could bring you much more.

- _Extra line shown (only if: dejima):_ Every year the Dutch on Dejima send the shogun a report of world news, and their ships bring European goods and books.
- _Extra line shown (only if: closed):_ Since your shogunate cut off all foreign trade, almost no foreign books reach Japan, and hardly anyone can read Dutch.
- _Extra line shown (only if: ban):_ Officials at Nagasaki still search every imported book for any mention of Christianity.

**A.** Let in books on science and medicine from abroad if they don't promote Christianity.  
**+5 points**  
_What actually happened:_ Close to what Yoshimune did in 1720: he eased the ban, letting in science books, including Chinese translations of European works, as long as they didn't promote Christianity. He also had scholars learn Dutch, and in 1774 Japanese doctors published a translation of a Dutch anatomy book, a landmark of "Dutch learning" (rangaku).

**B.** Keep the ban on foreign books. It has guarded Japan against Christian ideas since 1630, and the Chinese classics and Japan's own scholars offer plenty of wisdom.  
**+2 points**  
_What actually happened:_ Safe but limiting. Yoshimune eased the ban on non-religious books in 1720, and the "Dutch learning" that followed gave Japanese scholars valuable knowledge of medicine, astronomy, and geography.

**C.** Invite European teachers to open schools across Japan, missionaries included. Learning from real experts is faster than puzzling over books.  
**0 points** · stability -1  
_What actually happened:_ Letting missionaries back in would undo a century of policy and bring back the foreign influence the shogunate feared. Yoshimune allowed only non-religious books, brought in through Nagasaki by Chinese and Dutch traders.

#### General: The 47 Rōnin (1703)

> You keep order among the shogun's samurai. In 1701 Lord Asano drew his sword on Kira, a high official in charge of court ceremony, inside Edo Castle. Asano was ordered to commit seppuku and his lands were taken, but Kira went unpunished, and Asano's samurai became rōnin. Now forty-seven of them have broken into Kira's mansion, killed him, and laid his head at their lord's grave. They broke the shogun's law against private revenge, but many in Edo call them heroes.

**A.** Order them to commit seppuku. They die with honor as samurai, but the law against private feuds still stands.  
**+5 points**  
_What actually happened:_ That's what the shogunate decided in 1703, after officials and scholars argued over the case. The 46 rōnin who turned themselves in died by seppuku and were buried beside their lord, and their story became Japan's most famous tale of loyalty, retold in plays like Chūshingura.

**B.** Pardon them. Their loyalty to their dead lord is exactly the samurai virtue your peace depends on, and punishing heroes would turn Edo against you.  
**+1 point**  
_What actually happened:_ A pardon would tell every samurai that private revenge stood above the shogun's law, inviting more feuds. Some scholars did urge mercy, but the shogunate honored the rōnin's loyalty without excusing the killing.

**C.** Execute them as common criminals. Murder is murder, and samurai must learn that the shogun's law is above their sense of honor.  
**+2 points**  
_What actually happened:_ The law was upheld, but treating them as common murderers would have outraged samurai and townspeople who admired their loyalty. The shogunate let them die by seppuku, an honorable death, instead.

#### Senior Councilor: The Kyōhō Reforms (1722)

> As Senior Councilor to Shogun Yoshimune, you face an empty treasury. The shogunate lives mostly on rice taxes, but its costs keep rising, and it is struggling even to pay the stipends of its own retainers. Yoshimune wants practical reforms, and he wants to hear ideas from ordinary people, too.

- _Extra line shown (only if: alternate):_ Sankin-kōtai still requires every daimyo to spend every other year in Edo, at great cost to the lords.

**A.** Raise the rice tax and open new fields. Cut spending and set up a petition box for commoners. Let lords halve their time in Edo if they pay you rice. _(only if: alternate)_  
**+5 points**  
_What actually happened:_ These were Yoshimune's Kyōhō reforms. He raised rice taxes, encouraged new fields and crops like sweet potatoes, cut spending, set up a petition box in 1721, and from 1722 to 1730 let daimyo halve their stays in Edo in exchange for rice. The treasury recovered, though the higher taxes fell hard on farmers.

**A2.** Raise the rice tax and open new fields. Cut spending and set up a box where commoners can submit petitions. _(only if NOT: alternate)_  
**+4 points**  
_What actually happened:_ These were Yoshimune's Kyōhō reforms: higher rice taxes, new fields and crops, spending cuts, and a petition box in 1721. He also raised rice by letting daimyo shorten their stays in Edo, a deal that only works when sankin-kōtai is the law. The treasury recovered, though the higher taxes fell hard on farmers.

**B.** Mint new coins with less gold and silver in them. You can pay the bills right away, and no one has to pay higher taxes.  
**+2 points**  
_What actually happened:_ The shogunate had already debased its coins in 1695, which drove up prices, and the scholar Arai Hakuseki restored their quality in 1714. Yoshimune began with taxes, new fields, and spending cuts instead, though in 1736 he did remint the coins with less gold and silver to lift the low price of rice.

**C.** Borrow heavily from rich Osaka and Edo merchants, and worry about repaying them later.  
**0 points**  
_What actually happened:_ Borrowing would only push the problem into the future while interest piled up. Yoshimune raised income and cut spending instead.

## Ottoman Empire

**Tier:** Medium (-1 to +6 points per decision, starting stability 4). **Best possible team score:** 72.  
**Offices:** Ruler: Sultan · General: Pasha (General) · Vizier: Grand Vizier.  
**Regions on the map:** Anatolia (held at the start), Rumelia (the Balkans) (held at the start), Constantinople, Egypt and Syria, Hungary, Iraq.

### Round I: The Conqueror's City (1453–1514)

#### Sultan: The Walls of Constantinople (1453)

> As Sultan Mehmed II, only 21 years old, you rule lands on both sides of Constantinople, the last stronghold of the Byzantine Empire. Its thousand-year-old walls have stopped attackers for centuries, and a great chain blocks its harbor, the Golden Horn. A cannon-maker named Orban offers to cast guns bigger than any the world has seen. Taking the city would make you heir to the Roman emperors.

**A.** Hire Orban and build a fleet, then attack by land and sea at once. When the city falls, make it your capital and take the title Caesar of Rome.  
**+6 points** · sets flag: fleet · gains: Constantinople  
_What actually happened:_ This is what Mehmed did. Orban's cannons pounded the walls, ships were dragged overland on greased logs into the Golden Horn, and the city fell on May 29, 1453. Mehmed made it his capital and called himself Kayser-i Rum, "Caesar of Rome," claiming the Roman emperors' legacy.

**B.** Surround the city by land and starve it out. Those walls have held for centuries, so why waste soldiers when hunger can do the work?  
**+2 points**  
_What actually happened:_ Patient, but slow. Without warships to stop them, Venetian and Genoese ships could keep slipping food and soldiers into the city by sea. Mehmed won in under two months by pairing a land and sea blockade with huge cannons and a final assault.

**C.** Leave Constantinople alone. Its emperor rules little more than one city, so accept his gifts and conquer easier lands first.  
**-1 point**  
_What actually happened:_ A missed chance. An enemy capital in the middle of your lands could become a base for a European crusade. Mehmed made Constantinople his first great target, and its fall in 1453 shocked Europe and made his reputation.

#### Pasha (General): clash, Showdown at Chaldiran (1514)

See [Showdown at Chaldiran 1514](#showdown-at-chaldiran-1514) above.

#### Grand Vizier: A Half-Empty Capital (1454)

> As Grand Vizier, you must bring Constantinople back to life. After the siege, the great city, which many already call Istanbul, is half empty. Your sultan wants a busy capital full of merchants and craftsmen who pay taxes. The empire rules millions of Orthodox Christians, plus Armenians and Jews, alongside its Muslims. How will you fill the city, and how will you govern so many faiths?

**A.** Resettle Muslims, Christians, and Jews from across the empire, and let each faith run its own schools, courts, and charities under its own leaders.  
**+6 points** · sets flag: tolerant  
_What actually happened:_ This is close to what happened. Mehmed brought in people of all three faiths (many of them moved by force), named a new Orthodox patriarch, and let religious communities run much of their own affairs in return for loyalty and taxes. Historians later called this the millet system.

**B.** Fill the city with Muslim Turkish settlers only. A loyal Muslim capital would be easier to defend against a crusade, and Christians can stay in the countryside.  
**+1 point**  
_What actually happened:_ Workable, but you would lose skilled Greek, Armenian, and Jewish merchants and craftsmen. Mehmed deliberately brought in people of many faiths, and by the 1500s Istanbul was one of the largest cities in the world.

**C.** Order every Christian and Jew in the city to convert to Islam or leave.  
**-1 point** · stability -1  
_What actually happened:_ Forced conversion would spark revolts and drive away the taxpayers your capital needed. Mehmed did the opposite: he protected Christians and Jews in return for loyalty and the jizya, a tax paid by non-Muslims.

### Round II: The Magnificent Age (1516–1571)

#### Sultan: Keys to the Holy Cities (1517)

> As Sultan Selim I, you have just crushed the Mamluk Sultanate and taken Syria and Egypt, including Cairo, one of the richest cities in the Muslim world. Now the Sharif of Mecca sends his son to you with the keys of the holy city. Whoever protects Mecca and Medina, and the pilgrims who travel there on the hajj, wins enormous respect among Sunni Muslims. What will you do with your victory?

**A.** Accept the keys. Rule Egypt as a province, guard the pilgrim roads, and call yourself Servant of the Two Holy Cities.  
**+6 points** · sets flag: holy_cities · gains: Egypt and Syria  
_What actually happened:_ This is what Selim did. Protecting Mecca, Medina, and the hajj made the Ottoman sultan the leading ruler of the Sunni Muslim world, and Egypt's grain and taxes poured into Istanbul. Later sultans sometimes also called themselves caliph, leader of the Muslim community.

**B.** Load Cairo's treasure and its best craftsmen onto ships for Istanbul, then march home in triumph and leave Egypt and Arabia to whoever can hold them.  
**+1 point**  
_What actually happened:_ Selim did send treasure and many of Cairo's craftsmen to Istanbul, but he also kept Egypt. Its grain and taxes were worth far more than one load of loot, and walking away would have thrown away the prestige of guarding the holy cities.

**C.** Announce that every Muslim ruler, from Morocco to India, must now obey you directly, and send armies against any who refuse. As protector of the holy cities, you should lead them all.  
**-1 point**  
_What actually happened:_ Far too much at once. Rulers like the Shia Safavids in Persia and the sultans of Morocco had their own armies and their own claims to rule, and would never simply obey. The Ottomans led the wider Muslim world mainly through prestige: guarding Mecca, Medina, and the hajj.

#### Pasha (General): clash, The Battle of Lepanto (1571)

See [The Battle of Lepanto 1571](#the-battle-of-lepanto-1571) above.

#### Grand Vizier: Hungary After Mohács (1526)

> As Grand Vizier, you rode with Sultan Suleiman when his army crushed the Hungarians at the Battle of Mohács. Hungary's young king drowned while fleeing. Now two men claim his crown: John Zápolya, a Hungarian noble, and Ferdinand of Habsburg, brother of the mighty Emperor Charles V. Hungary is rich but far from Istanbul, and the Habsburgs will fight hard for it.

- _Extra line shown (only if: tolerant):_ Because your empire lets Christian communities keep their churches and leaders, some Hungarians may accept Ottoman rule more easily than the Habsburgs expect.

**A.** Back Zápolya as a friendly king who rules under your protection. If he fails, rule central Hungary yourself from Buda. Meanwhile, ally with France, the Habsburgs' great rival.  
**+6 points** · sets flag: france · gains: Hungary  
_What actually happened:_ This is what Suleiman and his advisers did. Zápolya ruled as an Ottoman vassal until he died in 1540, and in 1541 Suleiman took Buda and made central Hungary an Ottoman province for nearly 150 years. France became an ally against the Habsburgs in the 1530s.

**B.** March on Vienna every summer until the Habsburg capital falls. Without Vienna, the Habsburgs could never hold Hungary.  
**-1 point**  
_What actually happened:_ Vienna was too far away. Suleiman besieged it in 1529, but autumn rains, long supply lines, and stubborn defenders forced him to turn back. An army that marches every summer wears itself out; holding Hungary was the smarter goal.

**C.** Let Ferdinand have Hungary in return for peace and yearly gifts.  
**+1 point**  
_What actually happened:_ That would hand a rich kingdom, and a base for attacking your Balkan lands, to your strongest Christian rival. Suleiman fought for Hungary instead, and in 1547 the Habsburgs even agreed to pay him tribute for the slice they kept.

### Round III: Strains (1580–1650)

#### Sultan: The Sultan and the Janissaries (1622)

> As young Sultan Osman II, you are angry. The janissaries, once slave soldiers who served only the sultan, now marry, run shops, and pass their places to their sons. You blame them for last year's disappointing war against Poland. You want an army that obeys you, but the janissaries guard your palace, fill Istanbul's streets, and have forced sultans to sacrifice officials before.

**A.** Go slowly. Pay the troops on time and win over their officers. Change the army one step at a time so that no group feels suddenly threatened.  
**+6 points** · sets flag: army_reform  
_What actually happened:_ The road not taken. Osman II moved fast, and when word spread that he planned to raise a new army in Anatolia, the janissaries rebelled and killed him in 1622, the first Ottoman sultan murdered by his own soldiers. The janissaries were finally abolished in 1826, by a sultan who first made sure he had loyal troops and the support of religious leaders.

**B.** Strike first. Announce a pilgrimage to Mecca, slip away to Anatolia, and raise a new army there that owes everything to you. Then disband the janissaries.  
**-1 point** · stability -1  
_What actually happened:_ This was Osman II's plan, or so his enemies claimed. In May 1622 the janissaries rose up, dragged him from the palace, and killed him. A sudden purge without loyal troops ready was deadly.

**C.** Keep the peace by paying the janissaries a big bonus whenever they grumble, and leave their privileges alone. _(only if NOT: france)_  
**+2 points**  
_What actually happened:_ This bought quiet for a while, but bonuses emptied the treasury and taught the janissaries that rioting pays. Sultans after Osman II often gave in this way, and mutinies kept coming.

**C2.** Ask your old allies in France to send officers who can train a new army in the European style. France fears the Habsburgs as much as you do. _(only if: france)_  
**+1 point**  
_What actually happened:_ Christian officers drilling Muslim troops would have enraged the janissaries and religious scholars in 1622. A Frenchman did help a century later: Bonneval, a former French officer who became a Muslim, helped modernize the Ottoman bombardiers, an artillery corps, in the 1730s.

#### Pasha (General): Retaking Baghdad (1638)

> You command Sultan Murad IV's army. About fifteen years ago, Shah Abbas of Persia seized Baghdad, a rich trading city and the old capital of the caliphs. Ottoman armies have tried twice to win it back and failed. Now the sultan himself is marching east with a huge army and heavy siege cannons. Baghdad's walls are strong, and the war with Persia has dragged on ever since.

- _Extra line shown (only if: holy_cities):_ Because your sultans protect Mecca and Medina and claim to lead Sunni Muslims, leaving Baghdad and its famous Sunni shrines in Shia hands is an embarrassment.

**A.** Bring the heavy siege guns and enough food and powder for a long siege. Once Baghdad falls, offer the shah a fixed border and a lasting peace.  
**+6 points** · sets flag: zuhab · gains: Iraq  
_What actually happened:_ This is what happened. Murad IV's cannons broke Baghdad's walls in 1638, and the Treaty of Zuhab (1639) drew a border between the two empires that lasted, with small changes, for centuries. It is close to the border between Iran and Iraq today.

**B.** Let the governors of the eastern provinces besiege Baghdad each summer with their own troops, and keep the sultan's main army safe for wars in Europe.  
**+2 points**  
_What actually happened:_ Smaller armies had already tried this, in 1625–1626 and again in 1630, and failed. Baghdad's walls and its Persian defenders were too strong for anything less than a full campaign with heavy guns.

**C.** After Baghdad falls, keep marching. Push deep into Persia, take the shah's capital at Isfahan, and finish off the Safavids for good.  
**-1 point** · stability -1  
_What actually happened:_ Ottoman armies had invaded Persia many times but never held its heartland, because scorched earth and long supply lines wore them down. Endless war drained both empires, which is why both sides accepted peace in 1639.

#### Grand Vizier: Bad Coins, Angry Soldiers (1589)

> As Grand Vizier, you face a money crisis. Silver from Spanish America is flowing into the empire's markets, and prices keep rising. To stretch its budget, the government has cut the amount of silver in the akçe, its main coin, so soldiers' pay buys less. This year, janissaries paid in the new coins rioted until the sultan handed over two top officials to be executed.

- _Extra line shown (only if: holy_cities):_ Egypt's grain and taxes still flow to Istanbul, but even Egypt's wealth cannot keep up with rising prices.

**A.** Stop cutting the silver and pay the troops in good coins. Raise money by selling tax-collecting contracts, with inspectors watching the collectors closely.  
**+6 points** · sets flag: sound_money  
_What actually happened:_ The wise path, but the government managed it only in fits and starts. Bad coins and late pay led to more mutinies and fed the Celali revolts in Anatolia (about 1595–1610). Tax farming did raise cash, but it worked best where officials kept collectors from squeezing peasants.

**B.** Raise taxes sharply on peasant farmers. They are the empire's biggest group of taxpayers, and the soldiers must be paid now.  
**+1 point**  
_What actually happened:_ Heavy new taxes brought in money but drove many peasants off their land. Some joined the bandit armies of the Celali revolts, which tore through Anatolia from about 1595 to 1610.

**C.** Mix even more copper into the coins. Each coin will buy less, but the mint can make more of them and pay every soldier on time.  
**-1 point** · stability -1  
_What actually happened:_ Debasing the coins again would push prices even higher, and soldiers notice when their pay buys less. Pay in bad coins is exactly what set off this year's riot.

### Round IV: Revival and Retreat (1656–1699)

#### Sultan: A Vizier with Full Powers (1656)

> As Sultan Mehmed IV, still a teenager, you rule an empire in crisis. The treasury is empty, and this spring soldiers angry about their pay rioted and hanged officials from a plane tree in Istanbul. A Venetian fleet now blocks the Dardanelles, the strait leading to your capital. Grand viziers have come and gone every few months. Your mother suggests a tough old governor, Köprülü Mehmed Pasha, but he has conditions.

- _Extra line shown (only if: sound_money):_ Because your team once stopped cutting the silver in the coins, merchants still trust Ottoman money, which gives a strong minister something to build on.
- _Extra line shown (only if: army_reform):_ Because the army was reformed slowly and paid on time, some regiments still trust the palace and may back a tough new minister.

**A.** Accept his conditions: full power to appoint and punish officials, with no meddling from the palace.  
**+6 points** · stability +1  
_What actually happened:_ This is what happened. Köprülü Mehmed Pasha ruthlessly executed rebels and corrupt officials, broke the Venetian blockade in 1657, and restored order, and his son continued his work. The Köprülü years even brought new conquests, such as Crete in 1669.

**B.** Keep replacing grand viziers until one succeeds. Never let one minister grow too powerful, and let your palace advisers weigh in on every appointment.  
**-1 point** · stability -1  
_What actually happened:_ This is how the empire had gotten into trouble: weak, short-lived grand viziers who could not fix anything before rivals pushed them out. The turnaround came only when one minister got real power.

**C.** Spend what is left in the treasury to pay off the rioters, then end the costly war with Venice by giving up your claim to the island of Crete.  
**+2 points**  
_What actually happened:_ Paying off rioters only teaches them that rioting works, and giving up Crete after eleven years of war would look weak. Under the Köprülüs, the Ottomans won Crete in 1669.

#### Pasha (General): Vienna, Once More (1683)

> You command the frontier troops in Hungary. A huge Ottoman army has marched north to fight the Habsburgs and help Imre Thököly, a Hungarian rebel against them. At the war council, Grand Vizier Kara Mustafa wants to go straight to Vienna and take the city that Suleiman could not. The peace treaty with the Habsburgs has not quite run out, and the Pope is urging Christian rulers to unite against you.

- _Extra line shown (only if: france):_ Your old ally France is again an enemy of the Habsburgs, and King Louis XIV will not send soldiers to help Vienna.
- _Extra line shown (only if: army_reform):_ Thanks to your team's careful army reforms, your troops are better paid and disciplined, but discipline cannot shorten a supply line hundreds of miles long.

**A.** Advise against Vienna. Repair and garrison the fortresses of Hungary, and hold what the empire already has.  
**+6 points**  
_What actually happened:_ The wise choice. Kara Mustafa besieged Vienna for two months in 1683 until a relief army led by King Jan Sobieski of Poland crushed his army. In the long war that followed, the Ottomans lost most of Hungary at the Treaty of Karlowitz (1699).

**B.** March on Vienna at once with every soldier and gun. Taking the Habsburg capital would finish Suleiman's work and make the army, and the grand vizier, legendary.  
**-1 point** · stability -1 · loses: Hungary  
_What actually happened:_ This is what happened, and it went badly. On September 12, 1683, Sobieski's relief army smashed the besiegers, Kara Mustafa was executed for the defeat, and a Holy League of Christian powers drove the Ottomans out of most of Hungary.

**C.** Send money and troops to Thököly's Hungarian rebels, and let them wear down the Habsburgs while the main army waits safely at Buda.  
**+2 points**  
_What actually happened:_ The Ottomans did back Thököly, whom they had recognized as king of Upper Hungary in 1682. But his rebels could not beat the Habsburgs alone, and Kara Mustafa's march on Vienna turned a frontier war into a disaster.

#### Grand Vizier: Peace at Karlowitz (1699)

> As Grand Vizier, you inherit a long, losing war. Since the failed siege of Vienna in 1683, a Holy League of Austria, Poland, Venice, and later Russia has beaten Ottoman armies again and again, most recently at Zenta in 1697. At Karlowitz, the Habsburgs and their allies offer peace, but the empire must give up most of Hungary and other lands. European armies now drill and fight in new ways.

- _Extra line shown (only if: zuhab):_ Thanks to the lasting peace with Persia signed at Zuhab, your eastern border is quiet, so at least you are not fighting on two fronts.
- _Extra line shown (only if NOT: zuhab):_ With no settled border in the east, Persia could strike while your armies are tied down in Europe.

**A.** Sign the treaty. Then send envoys to study how Europeans run their armies, their diplomacy, and inventions like the printing press.  
**+6 points**  
_What actually happened:_ This is close to what happened. The Ottomans signed at Karlowitz in 1699, their first major permanent loss of land to European powers, and in the following decades sent an ambassador to Paris (1720) and approved the first Ottoman printing press for Turkish books (1727). The empire lasted until 1922.

**B.** Refuse the treaty and fight on until one great victory wins Hungary back. Giving up land to Christian kings would shame the sultan before the whole empire.  
**-1 point** · stability -1  
_What actually happened:_ Pride over survival. The army had lost battle after battle and the treasury was exhausted, so fighting on would likely have cost even more land. The Ottomans signed at Karlowitz in 1699 and lived to fight another day.

**C.** Sign the treaty, but execute the commanders who lost as a warning, and keep the army exactly as it is.  
**+2 points**  
_What actually happened:_ Punishing individuals doesn't fix an army that has fallen behind. Ottoman reformers later argued that the empire had to learn from Europe, not just blame its generals.

## Russian Empire

**Tier:** Medium (-1 to +6 points per decision, starting stability 4). **Best possible team score:** 71.  
**Offices:** Ruler: Grand Prince, later Tsar · General: Voivode (General) · Vizier: Chief Minister.  
**Regions on the map:** Muscovy (held at the start), Novgorod, Kazan, Astrakhan, Siberia, Left-bank Ukraine, The Baltic coast.

### Round I: Gathering the Lands (1478–1556)

#### Grand Prince, later Tsar: The Great Stand on the Ugra (1480)

> As Grand Prince Ivan III of Moscow, you have gathered many Russian lands under your rule. For more than two centuries, Russian princes paid tribute to the Mongol khans of the Golden Horde, but the Horde is breaking apart and you have stopped paying. Now Khan Akhmat has marched north to demand it. His army waits across the Ugra River, and the king of Poland-Lithuania has promised to join him.

**A.** Refuse to pay. Guard every river crossing with troops and cannons, and let the khan's army sit in the cold.  
**+6 points** · sets flag: sovereign  
_What actually happened:_ That's essentially what happened. The two armies faced each other across the Ugra for weeks, the help from Poland-Lithuania never came, and in November the khan's army turned back. Historians often date the end of Mongol rule over Russia to this "Great Stand" of 1480.

**B.** Pay this one last time. A single payment costs far less than a war, and the Horde is falling apart anyway; once it collapses, you can stop paying without risking a battle.  
**+1 point**  
_What actually happened:_ Paying would have kept Moscow a junior partner of the Mongols and invited more demands. Ivan III held firm instead, and the khan's army retreated from the Ugra without a major battle.

**C.** Cross the Ugra and attack the khan's camp before his allies arrive.  
**-1 point** · stability -1  
_What actually happened:_ A pitched battle could have wiped out Moscow's army in a single day, and crossing a river to attack is especially dangerous. Ivan III wisely held the crossings, and the khan retreated in 1480 without a major battle.

#### Voivode (General): The Siege of Kazan (1552)

> You command the armies of Ivan IV, crowned the first "Tsar of All Russia" in 1547. To the east, the Muslim Khanate of Kazan controls the middle Volga River, a great trade route, and its raiders carry off Russian captives to sell as slaves. For decades Moscow has tried to rule Kazan through friendly khans, and they keep being overthrown. The tsar has given you siege cannons and his new musketeers, the streltsy.

**A.** Besiege Kazan with heavy cannons, gunpowder mines dug under its walls, and the streltsy. Then send your army on down the Volga to Astrakhan.  
**+6 points** · sets flag: streltsy · gains: Kazan, Astrakhan  
_What actually happened:_ That's what happened. Russian engineers blew holes in Kazan's walls with gunpowder mines, the city fell in 1552, and Astrakhan was annexed in 1556, giving Russia the whole Volga down to the Caspian Sea. Ivan built St. Basil's Cathedral in Moscow to celebrate.

**B.** Put another friendly khan on Kazan's throne and make him pay tribute. Ruling a big Muslim city would tie down an army for years.  
**+2 points**  
_What actually happened:_ Moscow had already tried this several times, and its friendly khans kept being overthrown by their own nobles. Ivan conquered Kazan in 1552 and Astrakhan in 1556 instead, taking control of the whole Volga trade route.

**C.** Leave the east alone and march west to win a port on the Baltic Sea.  
**-1 point**  
_What actually happened:_ Ivan did start a war for the Baltic in 1558, the Livonian War, and lost it after about 25 years. Taking Kazan first, as he actually did in 1552, removed a dangerous enemy and won the Volga trade.

#### Chief Minister: Swallowing Novgorod (1478)

> As Chief Minister to Ivan III, you must decide what to do with Novgorod, the rich trading republic Moscow has just annexed. Novgorod sold furs and wax to German merchants of the Hanseatic League and was ruled for centuries by its own assembly and a few great boyar families. Some of those families once asked Lithuania for help against Moscow. Ivan has already carried Novgorod's assembly bell off to Moscow.

**A.** Move Novgorod's old boyar and merchant families to other towns, and give their lands to loyal servicemen to hold only as long as they serve.  
**+6 points** · sets flag: pomestie · gains: Novgorod  
_What actually happened:_ That's what Ivan III did. In the 1480s he deported thousands of Novgorod's boyars and merchants and gave their lands to his servicemen as pomestie, estates held only in return for service. These estates bound a new class of gentry tightly to Moscow, and such service estates became the basis of Russia's cavalry army.

**B.** Leave the assembly and the great families in charge, as long as they swear loyalty and pay their taxes. A proud, rich city that runs itself will pay more than one held down by force.  
**+1 point**  
_What actually happened:_ Novgorod's leaders had already turned to Lithuania once, and Moscow had to defeat them in battle in 1471. Ivan III ended the city's self-rule and handed its lands to his own servicemen.

**C.** Burn Novgorod to the ground so it can never rebel again.  
**-1 point**  
_What actually happened:_ Destroying a rich trading city would throw away the wealth you just won. Ivan III kept Novgorod and its trade but replaced its leaders with his own men; it was his grandson Ivan IV who later led his men to sack the city in 1570.

### Round II: Terror and Troubles (1565–1613)

#### Grand Prince, later Tsar: The Tsar's Terror (1565)

> As Tsar Ivan IV, you trust almost no one. Your beloved wife Anastasia died in 1560, and you suspect the boyars, Russia's great noble families, of poisoning her. Your war for the Baltic is going badly, and one of your top commanders, Prince Andrei Kurbsky, has fled to the enemy. In protest, you left Moscow and threatened to give up the throne unless you get a free hand against traitors.

- _Extra line shown (only if: pomestie):_ Your team's pomestie estates have created thousands of gentry servicemen who owe their land to the tsar, not to the old boyar clans.
- _Extra line shown (only if: sovereign):_ Since the Great Stand on the Ugra, Moscow's ruler has bowed to no khan, and the Church crowned you as God's chosen tsar.

**A.** Rely on your pomestie gentry and the councils. Punish proven traitors in open trials under your 1550 law code. _(only if: pomestie)_  
**+6 points**  
_What actually happened:_ Ivan did turn to lesser gentry, but as oprichniki, the black-clad horsemen of his oprichnina (1565–1572), who terrorized the boyars, killed thousands, and wrecked the economy. Working through councils and courts would have built a far steadier state.

**D.** Rule with the boyar council and the Church, and punish proven traitors in open trials under your 1550 law code. _(only if NOT: pomestie)_  
**+6 points**  
_What actually happened:_ That would have built a stronger, steadier state. Instead Ivan created the oprichnina (1565–1572), a private territory policed by black-clad horsemen who terrorized the boyars, killed thousands, and wrecked the economy. It is a big reason he is remembered as "Ivan the Terrible."

**B.** Split the realm in two. In your half, a black-clad private army will hunt traitors and seize boyar lands; if the boyars really poisoned Anastasia, only fear will keep the rest in line.  
**-1 point** · stability -1 · sets flag: terror  
_What actually happened:_ This is what Ivan really did. His oprichnina (1565–1572) killed thousands, including in the sack of Novgorod in 1570, and it could not even stop Crimean Tatars from burning Moscow in 1571.

**C.** Step back from ruling and let the boyar council govern in your name, as it did when you were a boy, so the nobles have no reason to plot.  
**+1 point**

## Spanish Empire

### Round I: A New World (1492–1524)

#### King or Queen: A Sailor's Wild Idea (1492)

> As Queen Isabella of Castile, ruling Spain with your husband, King Ferdinand of Aragon, you have just completed the Reconquista: in January 1492 your armies took Granada, the last Muslim kingdom in Spain. **+8 points** · sets flag: atlantic · gains: The Caribbean  
_What actually happened:_ A cheap bet that changed history. Columbus never reached Asia; he landed in the Bahamas and the Caribbean in October 1492, but his voyages opened the Americas to Spanish conquest. Within a century, American silver was paying for Spain's armies across Europe.

**B.** Refuse. Your experts have checked his numbers: Asia is far too distant to reach by sailing west, and his crews would run out of food and water long before they saw land.  
**+1 point**  
_What actually happened:_ The experts were right about the distance, but no one in Europe knew that two continents lay in the way. Ferdinand and Isabella funded Columbus, and his landing in the Caribbean in 1492 began Spain's empire in the Americas.

**C.** Turn him down, and put all your effort into making Spain fully Catholic by forcing its Jews and Muslims to convert or leave.  
**-2 points** · stability -1  
_What actually happened:_ In 1492 Ferdinand and Isabella really did order Spain's Jews to convert or leave; tens of thousands were driven into exile, losing homes and businesses, and Castile's Muslims faced the same choice in 1502. **B.** Let Cortés and his captains rule Mexico as lords of the lands they won. They risked their lives, they know the country, and governing it from across an ocean would be slow and costly.  
**+1 point**  
_What actually happened:_ Dangerous: conquistadors far from Spain could become independent kings, and in the 1540s Peru's conquistadors did rebel against the Crown. Their encomiendas, grants of Indigenous people's labor, also led to terrible abuse of a population already devastated by disease.

**C.** Recall Cortés in chains for defying the governor of Cuba, and abandon Mexico.  
**-2 points**  
_What actually happened:_ Abandoning Mexico would have thrown away what became the richest part of Spain's empire, where huge silver mines were found in the 1540s. Charles instead made Cortés governor in 1522, then sent royal officials to keep him in check.

#### Chief Minister: Ruling the Indies (1503)

> As Chief Minister to Isabella and Ferdinand, you must decide how to control Spain's new lands across the Atlantic, which Spaniards call the Indies, and the trade that flows to and from them. _What actually happened:_ That's what Spain built. **B.** Let any merchant sail to the Indies and trade freely, without licenses or records. Trade will grow faster, and the Crown can tax goods when they land.  
**+1 point**  
_What actually happened:_ Without licenses and records, much of the gold and silver would have been smuggled past the Crown's officials. Spain instead sent colonial trade through the Casa de Contratación in Seville (1503), which licensed ships and recorded their cargoes.

**C.** Lease whole colonies to rich bankers in return for loans. They pay for the ships and settlers, and the Crown takes no risk.  
**-2 points**  
_What actually happened:_ Charles V actually tried this in 1528, handing Venezuela to the Welsers, a German banking family he owed money. Their agents raided for gold and enslaved Indigenous people, and the Crown later took the colony back.

**B.** Storm the walls with cavalry before the floods rise.  
**-2 points** · stability -1  
_What actually happened:_ Horses can't charge across a flooded plain or climb city walls. Sonni Ali used his river fleet to besiege Jenne until it surrendered around 1473.

**C.** Accept a yearly tribute from Jenne and leave the city free, since a long siege could tie up your army for years while rivals grow stronger elsewhere.  
**+2 points**  
_What actually happened:_ Tribute brings in gold, but an independent Jenne could switch sides later. Sonni Ali took the city around 1473 and made it part of Songhai.

### Round II: Askia Muhammad (1493–1528)

#### King (Sonni, later Askia): A Throne Without Royal Blood (1496)

> As Askia Muhammad, you were one of Sonni Ali's top generals. In 1493 you defeated his son in battle and seized the throne, taking the title askia. You are a devout Muslim, but you are not of royal blood, and many people see you as a usurper. Sonni Ali's family and its supporters are waiting for you to stumble.

- _Extra line shown (only if: scholars_loyal):_ Timbuktu's scholars remember that Songhai protected them under Sonni Ali, and they are ready to support you.
- _Extra line shown (only if NOT: scholars_loyal):_ Timbuktu's scholars still remember how Sonni Ali treated them, and they want proof that you are different.

**A.** Go on the hajj to Mecca, appoint Muslim judges, and support the scholars of Timbuktu.  
**+8 points** · stability +1 · sets flag: legitimate  
_What actually happened:_ This is what Askia Muhammad did. On his hajj in 1496–1497 he was named the caliph's deputy for West Africa, and at home he appointed Muslim judges and generously supported Timbuktu's scholars. Islam gave his rule a legitimacy that royal blood could not.

**B.** Rely on your army. It won you the throne, and soldiers who share in the spoils of war will keep you there.  
**-2 points** · stability -1  
_What actually happened:_ An army can seize a throne, but keeping it takes legitimacy, and a general who wins by force invites the next general to try. Askia Muhammad built his legitimacy on Islam, the hajj, and the support of Timbuktu's scholars.

**C.** Win over the clan chiefs and Sonni Ali's old commanders with rich gifts and high offices, so the most powerful men in Songhai have every reason to accept you.  
**+2 points**  
_What actually happened:_ Gifts and offices can buy time, but loyalty that is bought can be bought again by a rival. Askia Muhammad built a deeper base by making Islam central to his rule.

#### General: West to Mali, East to Aïr (1500)

> You command Askia Muhammad's armies. To the west lie lands once ruled by the old empire of Mali. Far to the east, in the Aïr Mountains, the sultan of Agadez controls a crossroads of caravan routes across the Sahara. To the south, the Mossi kingdoms have raided Songhai lands before, and farther south lie the gold fields that make West Africa rich.

- _Extra line shown (only if: river_fleet):_ Songhai's war canoes control the Niger, so troops and supplies can move quickly by river.
- _Extra line shown (only if NOT: river_fleet):_ Songhai has no strong river fleet, so every army must march and carry its own supplies.

**A.** Move troops by canoe along the Niger and take the trade towns west and east. Let local rulers keep their thrones if they pay tribute. _(only if: river_fleet)_  
**+8 points** · gains: The western Sahel, Air and Agadez  
_What actually happened:_ Close to what Askia Muhammad did. His armies campaigned west into Mali's old lands and east into Aïr, where the sultan of Agadez kept his throne but paid Songhai tribute. Songhai's war canoes made the Niger a highway for its armies.

**D.** Take the trade towns west and east, and let local rulers keep their thrones if they pay tribute. _(only if NOT: river_fleet)_  
**+6 points** · gains: The western Sahel, Air and Agadez  
_What actually happened:_ Close to what Askia Muhammad did: his armies campaigned west into Mali's old lands and east into Aïr, where the sultan of Agadez paid Songhai tribute. Without war canoes to carry supplies on the Niger, though, every campaign is slower and costlier.

**B.** March south to conquer the Mossi kingdoms and seize the gold fields beyond them, so the gold flows straight to Gao instead of through other people's markets.  
**-2 points** · stability -1  
_What actually happened:_ Askia Muhammad did attack the Mossi in 1497–1498, calling it a holy war, but the Mossi kingdoms were never conquered. The gold fields lay far to the south and southwest, beyond the reach of Songhai's cavalry.

**C.** Conquer the new lands and replace every local ruler with one of your own officers. No one will rebel against men loyal only to you.  
**+1 point**  
_What actually happened:_ Distant peoples resent foreign officers, and holding them down takes a huge army. Askia Muhammad sent his own governors to key provinces but often left local rulers in place if they paid tribute.

#### Chief Minister: Organizing an Empire (1510)

> As Askia Muhammad's chief minister, you must run an empire that now stretches across much of West Africa. Its peoples speak many languages, and merchants from North Africa and Egypt crowd the markets of Gao and Timbuktu. Far to the north, in the middle of the Sahara, lie the salt mines of Taghaza. West Africa has little salt of its own, and Taghaza's salt trades for gold.

- _Extra line shown (only if: river_fleet):_ The war canoes that took Jenne now guard the river trade between Gao, Timbuktu, and Jenne.

**A.** Appoint governors, keep a full-time army, set standard weights and measures, and send officials to run the Taghaza mines.  
**+8 points** · sets flag: admin · gains: Taghaza salt mines  
_What actually happened:_ These were Askia Muhammad's reforms. Appointed governors, a full-time army, and fair rules for trade held the huge empire together, and Songhai officials oversaw the salt of Taghaza. Timbuktu's Sankore mosque drew scholars from across West and North Africa.

**B.** Let local chiefs rule as they wish, as long as they send tribute. They know their own peoples, and fewer outsiders means fewer revolts.  
**+1 point**  
_What actually happened:_ Cheap, but loose control lets chiefs break away. Askia Muhammad appointed his own governors and built a professional army to hold the empire together.

**C.** Double the taxes on caravans. The merchants are rich, and the army needs pay.  
**-2 points**  
_What actually happened:_ Heavy taxes can push caravans onto other routes across the Sahara. Askia Muhammad encouraged trade instead, and Songhai's markets drew merchants from across the desert.

### Round III: Golden Age (1528–1582)

#### King (Sonni, later Askia): Brothers and the Throne (1528)

> In 1528 Askia Muhammad, old and nearly blind, was overthrown by his own son Musa. Now you rule as Askia Musa. Your father had many sons, and several of your brothers command soldiers and have followers of their own. Songhai has no fixed rule for who inherits the throne, so any prince with an army may try to seize it.

- _Extra line shown (only if: admin):_ Askia Muhammad's system of appointed governors gives you important posts to offer your brothers.
- _Extra line shown (only if: legitimate):_ Because Askia Muhammad made Islam central to his rule, princes and soldiers respect the judgment of Timbuktu's judges and scholars.

**A.** Have the scholars and top commanders agree on a clear rule for who inherits. Give your brothers governorships far from Gao.  
**+8 points** · stability +1 · sets flag: succession_rule  
_What actually happened:_ The road not taken. Askia Musa was killed in a revolt in 1531, and Songhai's princes kept fighting over the throne for the rest of the century, which weakened the army.

**B.** Let the princes fight it out when the time comes. The strongest deserves the throne.  
**-3 points** · stability -1  
_What actually happened:_ This is roughly what happened. Repeated fights between princes, ending in a civil war in 1588, weakened Songhai's army right before Morocco invaded.

**C.** Name no heir and keep every brother hoping. As long as each believes he might be chosen, none of them has a reason to rebel against you.  
**+1 point**  
_What actually happened:_ Without a named heir, ambitious princes build their own followings and wait for a chance. In Songhai, rival princes overthrew one another again and again after 1528.

#### General: Raiders at the Salt Mines (1557)

> You command Songhai's forces on the northern frontier. Morocco's sultans want the salt mines of Taghaza, deep in the Sahara, and a Moroccan raiding party has just struck the mines, killing some of the people there. Salt from Taghaza is traded for gold in Songhai's markets, but the mines are weeks of desert travel from Gao.

- _Extra line shown (only if: admin):_ Askia Muhammad's officials still oversee the Taghaza mines, and his full-time army gives you trained soldiers to send north.
- _Extra line shown (only if NOT: admin):_ Songhai never put its own officials at Taghaza, so news from the desert reaches you late and secondhand.

**A.** Patrol the caravan routes with horsemen and Tuareg allies. If Taghaza falls, help the salt workers open mines farther south.  
**+8 points**  
_What actually happened:_ A sound defense, and salt kept reaching Songhai's markets. Moroccan raiders struck Taghaza in the 1550s, and after Morocco seized it in the 1580s, salt workers moved to new mines farther south at Taoudenni.

**B.** Let Morocco have Taghaza. It is weeks away, and traders will still bring salt to your markets.  
**+1 point** · loses: Taghaza salt mines  
_What actually happened:_ Giving ground did not satisfy Morocco, whose sultans really wanted Songhai's gold. In 1590 Morocco sent an army across the Sahara to take it.

**C.** Lead your whole army north across the Sahara and strike Morocco itself. A bold blow at the sultan's own cities will teach him never to touch Songhai's mines again.  
**-3 points** · loses: Taghaza salt mines  
_What actually happened:_ Crossing the Sahara would cost thousands of men to heat and thirst, as Morocco's own army learned in 1590–1591. Meanwhile the salt mines would be left unguarded.

#### Chief Minister: Guns from the North (1578)

> As chief minister to Askia Daoud, you serve a rich empire. Gold from the south and salt from the Sahara pass through Songhai's markets, and Timbuktu's scholars are famous across West and North Africa. But in 1578 Morocco crushed a Portuguese invasion at the Battle of the Three Kings, and its new sultan, Ahmad al-Mansur, is building an army of musketeers. Songhai's soldiers still fight with spears, bows, and cavalry.

**A.** Buy guns and hire gunners through North African traders, and train Songhai soldiers to use them.  
**+8 points** · sets flag: guns  
_What actually happened:_ The road not taken, though Bornu, near Lake Chad, did hire Turkish musketeers in these years. Songhai's army never adopted guns in large numbers, and when Moroccan musketeers reached the Niger in 1591, that gap proved costly.

**B.** Trust the Sahara. No army has ever crossed it to attack Songhai, and the sultan's musketeers would die of thirst long before they reached the Niger.  
**-3 points** · stability -1  
_What actually happened:_ Many in Songhai believed the desert would protect them. In 1590–1591 a Moroccan army crossed it, and its guns proved decisive.

**C.** Spend the gold on Timbuktu: new mosques, libraries, and rich gifts for scholars. Songhai's fame as a home of learning is its greatest strength.  
**+2 points**  
_What actually happened:_ Askia Daoud was a generous patron of Timbuktu's scholars, and the city flourished. But learning could not stop bullets, and Songhai's army was still unprepared when Morocco's musketeers arrived in 1591.

### Round IV: The Moroccan Invasion (1582–1591)

#### King (Sonni, later Askia): Brother Against Brother (1588)

> As Askia Ishaq II, you face a rebellion. Your brother Muhammad al-Sadiq, a top army commander, has declared himself askia and is marching on Gao, and many soldiers have joined him. Meanwhile Morocco's sultan, Ahmad al-Mansur, is demanding taxes from the Taghaza salt mines, a sign that he has his eye on Songhai.

- _Extra line shown (only if: succession_rule):_ Because Songhai has a clear rule for who inherits, most commanders and scholars see the rebel as a lawbreaker, and fewer soldiers have joined him.
- _Extra line shown (only if NOT: succession_rule):_ With no fixed rule for who inherits, many soldiers believe the rebel has as good a claim as you do.

**A.** Strike fast and crush the rebellion. Then pardon the rebel soldiers and bring them back into your army.  
**+8 points**  
_What actually happened:_ Ishaq II did defeat the rebels in 1588, but the fighting cost Songhai many soldiers and left the army bitter and divided. Pardoning and reuniting the army would have left Songhai stronger when the Moroccans came.

**B.** Execute every rebel you capture. If traitors are forgiven, every ambitious prince in Songhai will raise an army, believing he has nothing to lose.  
**-1 point**  
_What actually happened:_ Killing your own soldiers weakens your army just when you need it most. The civil war of 1588 had already cost Songhai many fighting men before Morocco attacked.

**C.** Make peace by splitting the empire: the rebel takes the west, and you keep Gao and the east.  
**-3 points** · stability -1  
_What actually happened:_ A divided Songhai would be even easier for Morocco to conquer. Ishaq II won the civil war in 1588 and kept the empire together, though it was weakened.
